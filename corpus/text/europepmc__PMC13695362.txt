Essential thrombocythemia (ET) is a chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by sustained thrombocytosis and an increased risk of thrombosis and hemorrhage. 1 , 2 Vascular complications, as well as the risk of disease progression to myelofibrosis or acute myeloid leukemia (AML), represent the primary sources of mortality and morbidity among individuals with ET. 3 Consequently, the primary goals of ET treatment are to prevent vascular events and avert disease progression. 4 In Sweden, the incidence of ET is estimated at 1.6 per 100,000 individuals, 5 with a prevalence of approximately 30 per 100,000 individuals, 1 , 6 and a median age of 69 years at diagnosis, 7 highlighting a rare but chronic condition with long-term clinical consequences.

The humanistic burden of ET is multifaceted, including long-term risk of vascular complications and disease progression alongside a considerable effect on individuals’ quality of life (QoL). 8–10 Commonly reported symptoms include fatigue, dizziness, and bone pain, which may persist over time and adversely impact cognitive and physical functioning, resulting in a substantial impact on individuals’ daily lives. 8–10 In addition to the humanistic impact, ET also imposes a considerable economic burden due to the costs associated with lifelong monitoring, cytoreductive therapy, management of thrombotic complications, and adverse drug effects. 11

The effect of ET is not constrained to the healthcare system, as individuals with ET report an impact on productivity, wages and overall labor force participation. 12–14 A Danish study of MPN patients demonstrated that individuals with ET experience lower rates of employment and higher rates of disability, unemployment and sickness benefit, when compared to a matched non-ET cohort. 14 This is supported by other studies which report a wide range of ET-associated economic effects such as lower salaries, reduced work hours, loss of employment and transitions to part-time employment. 13 , 15 Although the median age at diagnosis is relatively high, 7 a meaningful proportion of individuals with ET are still of working age and are therefore susceptible to these labor market effects. 16

While some studies have explored the indirect costs of ET, these do not consider the downstream effect on governments from lower rates of employment and higher rates of enrolment in social support programs. 12 Studies have also reported the impact of ET on employment-related outcomes; however, these do not quantify the impact of these employment outcomes on government finances. 14 This is particularly important in tax-funded healthcare systems such as Sweden, where the government represents the primary payer for healthcare, while also being impacted by adverse labor market outcomes. 17 The government analytic perspective or fiscal perspective represents a method for capturing the broader labor market impact of disease by linking economic outcomes with government revenue and expenditure. 18 , 19 This approach captures the relationship between government revenue from direct and indirect tax combined with spending on social support programs and has been utilized in several therapeutic areas to quantify the fiscal implications of the disease. 20–22 The objective of this study was to develop an economic model to assess the fiscal burden of disease of ET in Sweden.

A cohort-based model was developed in Microsoft Excel ® to assess the fiscal burden of ET in Sweden. Two hypothetical cohorts of 1,000 individuals were compared: one comprising individuals with ET and the other with age-matched members of the general Swedish population. For each cohort, lifetime healthcare costs and fiscal flows were generated from a given starting age. Cohort results were calculated by sampling 1,000 starting ages from a normal distribution to reflect published characteristics of the ET population (ie mean age and standard deviation) 16 and generating a weighted average of the corresponding age-specific results.

Fiscal flows were generated for each cohort to estimate government tax revenue from employment and income levels as well as government expenditure on healthcare resource use and social support programs. Fiscal data for the general Swedish population, such as age and gender-specific employment levels, income levels and recipients of social support programs, were sourced from the Statistics Sweden database. 23–26

For the cohort of individuals with ET, adjustments were applied to reflect elevated mortality, 4 , 27 reduced fiscal flows, 12–14 and excess healthcare resource use compared to the general Swedish population. 11 A targeted literature review was conducted to identify published data linking the presence of ET and relevant fiscal effects, such as change in employment, income loss, productivity losses, and long-term disability, with full details of this search provided in Supplemental Appendix 1 . The targeted literature review and supplemental hand searches identified several studies reporting the impact of ET on employment, 14 income, 13 early retirement, 13 productivity losses 12–14 and long-term disability. 14 Estimates from these studies were used to adjust national Swedish fiscal data to reflect the outcomes among individuals with ET. Reported outcomes were typically expressed as relative risks (RR), odds ratios (ORs) or proportions. The fiscal flows in this analysis can be broadly split into two categories to reflect elements contributing to government revenue and those relating to government expenditure. Government revenue is assumed to comprise direct and indirect tax revenue. Direct tax revenue is calculated using the Swedish tax wedge which represents the average proportion of gross labor costs that is taken in taxes by the Swedish government, and is reported by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). 28 The tax wedge is applied to employment income, both part-time and full-time, to calculate direct tax revenue from wages. ET can also lead to high rates of productivity losses, both for individuals with ET and their caregivers. 12–14 To capture this effect, lost productivity, both from absenteeism and presenteeism, was linked to direct tax loss by capturing the excess loss of workdays outlined in Table 1 . It was assumed that all employed individuals with ET experience absenteeism with 5.44 excess absent days attributable to ET each year. An alternative estimate of absenteeism prevalence, based on applying a published RR to general population prevalence, was explored as a scenario analysis. Due to limited available data on the productivity impact of ET-associated presenteeism, it was assumed that one day of presenteeism leads to a 50% loss in productivity (ie half of the loss of absenteeism), with alternative values tested in scenario analyses. This was considered a conservative estimate, as evidence in other disease areas indicates that presenteeism costs are comparable to, or exceed, those associated with absenteeism. 29 , 30 An absenteeism effect was applied to both individuals with ET and their caregivers, while presenteeism was only applied to individuals with ET. As there are few studies exploring caregiver demographics specifically related to ET, caregivers were assumed to be aged 62 in line with a published study of informal caregiving in Sweden. 31 To reflect that caregivers may change as individuals get older, caregiver age was kept constant in the model, and used to calculate age-adjusted employment rates and, subsequently, the associated caregiver productivity loss.

Indirect tax revenue reflects funds from taxes on goods and services collected at the point of consumption and was estimated by applying the Swedish value-added tax (VAT) rate to disposable income levels. 23 , 32 This assumes that all disposable income is spent on consumption for all age groups. Government expenditure was captured through rates of social support program use including unemployment allowances, early retirement pensions and disability payments.

In addition to capturing the fiscal impact of ET, the model also incorporated healthcare and mortality effects associated with ET. The healthcare burden was incorporated by estimating the excess healthcare resource use associated with ET. As it was not possible to identify a Swedish study providing a detailed breakdown of ET resource use, a Danish study was used. 11 This study was considered a reasonable proxy given that ET guidelines have been previously drafted to reflect a consistent approach across all Nordic countries. 33 This registry-based cohort study included individuals with MPN with a specific subgroup focusing on ET, and tracked healthcare resource use compared to a matched non-MPN cohort. 11 The annual excess healthcare resource use for the ET cohort was calculated based on average use reported over eight years post-diagnosis, 11 and combined with unit costs taken from the 2026 regional pricing and reimbursement list for the Southern Healthcare Region in Sweden. 34 The resource use and unit costs are described in Table 2 , and correspond to annual excess healthcare costs of SEK 64,542 for each individual with ET. These costs were applied to all living individuals in the ET cohort each year, and reflect a blended estimate of ET-attributable healthcare costs.

Excess mortality associated with ET was captured by applying a constant published hazard ratio of 1.22 to age and gender-adjusted mortality rates from Swedish life tables. 4 , 27

To model the cumulative fiscal burden of ET in Sweden, a one-off lifetime fiscal cost was calculated for individuals aged 18 to 95. This cost reflects the difference in lifetime tax revenue and government expenditure between the general population cohort and the ET cohort for each starting age. It was assumed that the maximum age of employment in Sweden is 69, with all individuals exiting the workforce from the age of 70 onwards. These lifetime costs were applied to a cohort of 1,000 individuals with ages sampled from a normal distribution reflecting published characteristics of the ET population (mean age of 65, standard deviation of 16). 16 A weighted result was calculated reflecting the age distribution in the sampled cohort and compared against a cohort reflecting the average Swedish population with the same age distribution (see Supplemental Appendix 3 for details on the sampled age distribution).

The primary calculations underpinning this analysis are informed by several key equations. These are described below with the full equations provided in Supplemental Appendix 2 .

To capture the relative effect of ET on employment, Swedish-level employment rates were calculated by combining the age-matched rates of economic activity among the overall population with employment rates among the economically active population. These were then adjusted by the published employment RR to reflect both overall employment rates and reduced rates of full-time employment among the ET cohort. Employment income was estimated by combining full-time and part-time employed individuals with the associated income levels, while lifetime income considered age-adjusted income up to the age of workforce exit, accounting for both wage growth and discount rates. A discount rate of 3% was applied to all costs, reflecting the rate recommended in Swedish economic evaluations. 35 For spending on social support programs (ie spending on unemployment allowances, early retirement pension, and disability pension), payments reflect the published age-adjusted prevalence of each program, the associated payment and the RR reflecting the ET effect. This is then discounted and adjusted for VAT to remove the proportion of the payment that the government will recover through consumption tax. Absenteeism, both for individuals with ET and their caregivers, was assumed to create a further loss of direct tax, based on the link between lost work hours, gross domestic product (GDP) loss per work hour, and the proportion of GDP attributable to tax. This assumes that when an individual experiences an absent day, eight hours of work are lost, at a GDP loss of SEK 872 per work hour (based on OECD estimates), 36 with 41.4% of this relating to tax. 37 This is combined with an assumed marginal loss of productivity of 50% to reflect that a worker's entire productivity will not be lost when they are absent, due to factors such as co-workers covering the absent individual's workload. 38 The marginal loss of productivity from an absent day was tested in scenario analyses.

The total direct tax loss attributable to ET reflects the tax loss from employment income (using the tax wedge), absenteeism and presenteeism for individuals with ET and absenteeism among caregivers, while indirect tax loss is based on consumption tax, estimated by applying the Swedish VAT rate to disposable income. To calculate the incremental fiscal impact of ET, the net fiscal effects were estimated based on the difference in government tax revenue and spending on social support programs for the cohort of ET individuals and the matched cohort of the general Swedish population. The total fiscal burden of ET equates to the incremental fiscal impact and excess healthcare costs between the two cohorts.

In addition to the base case analysis, a range of sensitivity analyses was conducted to assess model uncertainty. This included a one-way sensitivity analysis illustrating the uncertainty of individual parameters and a series of scenario analyses which are described in Table 3 .

This analysis explores the fiscal burden of ET by capturing the broader impact of ET on individuals’ ability to work. ET generates a substantial lifetime fiscal burden, driven by reduced employment, lower productivity, and an increased requirement for social support programs, all in addition to the healthcare burden. 11 , 12 , 14 Although ET is a relatively rare disease with an older age of onset, the clinical burden relating to vascular events and disease progression combined with the observed impact on economic outcomes suggests that there is a considerable fiscal burden borne by the Swedish government. This analysis shows that the proportion of the ET patient cohort who remain within the workforce incur a high fiscal impact. While not explored explicitly in this study, it is possible that these fiscal effects could be driven by the considerable symptom burden associated with ET, as symptoms such as fatigue are likely to influence individuals’ ability to work and sustain labor force participation. 40 While healthcare costs represented the single largest contributor to the overall fiscal burden of ET at SEK 890,298, this is closely followed by the total direct and indirect tax burden of SEK 766,665, demonstrating the significant costs that can be created by labor market effects. This tax burden reflects both the reduced employment and labor market outcomes among individuals with ET as well as the impact of ET-attributable mortality, with premature mortality leading to fewer life years spent in employment. The estimated fiscal burden of SEK 1,721,734 is supported by a range of sensitivity analyses which show that, while certain variables such as ET healthcare costs and the ET-attributable employment effects have a reasonable impact on results, the model still shows a considerable fiscal impact when using conservative parameter values. While ET may represent a rare condition, the per-person fiscal burden calculated in this analysis represents a meaningful long‑term impact on public finances. These estimates are consistent with fiscal analyses conducted in other disease areas, where labor‑market effects and social transfers have been shown to contribute substantially to lifetime government costs, often at a scale comparable to direct healthcare expenditure. 20 , 21

There are several limitations associated with this analysis. The study combines a range of data sources from different countries which may not fully reflect the ET population in Sweden. In particular, fiscal modifiers relating to the impact of ET on income, part-time employment and retirement were taken from US studies while ET healthcare resource use was based on a Danish cohort study. 12–14 Data gaps also existed for several model inputs that necessitated assumptions, such as ET-attributable income loss and caregiver absenteeism effects. In order to compensate for any uncertainty associated with these inputs, relevant values and assumptions were tested in sensitivity analyses, which indicated these inputs were not primary drivers of model results. As such, while uncertainty in these parameters may influence the overall magnitude of the effect associated with living with ET, this would not modify the conclusions drawn from these results. This study also applies assumptions with respect to how tax revenue is generated. In particular, it was assumed that all disposable income would be spent on consumption and that this would not change by age. In reality, individuals may save or invest a portion of their income, therefore the indirect tax burden of ET may be slightly overestimated in this analysis. 41 The modeling approach presented here is broadly applicable to other countries with similar tax‑funded healthcare and social insurance systems, however the exact magnitude of the fiscal burden of ET will depend on different factors including employment and income levels, social support programs and healthcare resource use.

This study considers the aggregate fiscal impact of ET and does not capture the full socioeconomic burden or differences among specific subgroups. The ET population is heterogeneous, with observed differences in clinically relevant factors such as risk of thrombosis or mutation status. 7 There may be specific subgroups with a higher fiscal burden, for example, those at high risk of vascular complications, however, these subgroups are not explicitly modeled. While beyond the scope of this analysis, the ability to more granularly link symptoms, outcomes and population characteristics to fiscal outcomes may assist in understanding the ET subgroups with the greatest burden. This may also support the evaluation of new policies and interventions by illustrating the potential fiscal savings that could be provided. For example, published evidence suggests that delays in diagnosing ET can occur, 42 , 43 which is particularly relevant when considering that the initial years following disease onset are likely associated with the greatest fiscal consequences. Interventions aimed at earlier diagnosis of ET could yield fiscal returns by enabling timely treatment and reducing the labor market impact of undiagnosed ET, although this is beyond the scope of the current study and could be considered for future research.

In tax-funded health systems such as Sweden’s, adopting a government perspective is a particularly useful tool as the state is both the primary payer for healthcare and the main recipient of tax revenue generated by economic activity. 17 By explicitly modeling fiscal transactions between individuals with ET and the public sector, including taxes, social insurance benefits, and healthcare spending, this analysis aligns with public economic frameworks that seek to understand how health conditions affect the sustainability of welfare states. 44 This approach extends beyond the traditional healthcare perspective by focusing on the net effects to government accounts, capturing unfulfilled lifetime tax contributions and increased transfer payments associated with ET-related morbidity and premature exit from the labor force. The model provides decision-makers in tax-financed systems with policy-relevant evidence on how investments in earlier diagnosis, optimal ET management, and employment-supportive measures could simultaneously alleviate clinical burden and improve long-term public finances.

This study explored the burden of disease associated with ET in Sweden using a fiscal perspective. This perspective explores the impact of ET on both healthcare costs and the wider labor market and demonstrates how this ultimately impacts the government by reducing tax revenue and increasing spending. While the analysis showed a considerable healthcare burden for ET, an equivalent burden was found when considering the loss in government tax revenue from reduced employment outcomes and increased spending on social support programs such as early retirement pension and disability payments. Adopting a fiscal perspective can provide decision makers with a more holistic understanding of the burden of ET beyond the healthcare system alone, and highlights the potential value of interventions that improve clinical outcomes while supporting sustained labor force participation.
