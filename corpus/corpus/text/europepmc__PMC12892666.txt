In the healthcare system, physicians perform medical interventions and make numerous medical decisions regarding patients’ diagnoses and treatments every day. These decisions and interventions critically influence patient outcomes. However, in addition to their curative role, all types of medical interventions inherently carry certain risks. In the event of an undesirable outcome following a medical intervention, it is important to determine its cause from scientific, medical, ethical, and legal perspectives. In this context, distinguishing between “medical malpractice” and a “legally permitted risk” (complication) is essential although often challenging. Under contemporary understanding of law, physicians and healthcare personnel fulfill their duties within the framework of “permitted risk” [ 1 ]. However, the possibility of facing malpractice lawsuits due to medical errors represents a significant source of fear in physicians’ professional lives. As a result, physicians may engage in defensive medical practices, defined as “practice based primarily on fear of litigation rather than expected patient outcomes” [ 2 , 3 ].

Physicians’ fear of malpractice litigation (malpractice fear [MF]) is an important factor that can influence patient care by increasing the tendency toward defensive medicine [ 4 ]. MF may lead physicians to order unnecessary diagnostic tests or treatment interventions to protect themselves against potential malpractice lawsuits [ 5 ], resulting in excess testing, increased radiation exposure, and unnecessary medication administration [ 6 ]. Therefore, MF can also contribute to excessive medical expenditures [ 7 ]. In addition, MF can decrease physicians’ WM [ 8 – 11 ], posing a critical risk factor for the health system. Therefore, identifying and addressing the factors that reduce physicians’ WM is essential.

While the literature demonstrates that MF negatively affects physicians’ WM [ 8 – 11 ], it is important to determine factors that may moderate this relationship. Understanding such moderating factors may help guide interventions aimed at preventing low WM. Thus, the present study investigated the moderating role of occupational self-efficacy (OS-E) in the relationship between physicians’ MF and WM. OS-E was selected based on prior evidence of its positive effect on physicians’ WM [ 12 , 13 ]. The study is grounded in social cognitive theory, which posits that self-efficacy beliefs influence how individuals perceive and respond to stressors [ 12 ]. From this perspective, OS-E may reduce anxiety-related negative cognitive evaluations and support more resilient coping in the face of perceived occupational risks, thus mitigating the demotivating effects of MF.

Anesthesiologists were selected as the study population for several reasons. First, the risk of malpractice lawsuits is especially high in medical workers that manage high-risk patient groups. Anesthesiologists provide care for patients undergoing surgical procedures and those admitted to intensive care units. In a study by Savaş, anesthesiology ranked third, after gynecology and general surgery, among medical specialties most frequently involved in malpractice lawsuits in Turkey [ 14 ]. Second, while the literature has investigated MF and its associated factors in some other medical specialties—including cardiology [ 15 ], emergency medicine [ 16 ], and orthopedics [ 17 ]—studies focusing on anesthesiologists are lacking. Furthermore, if OS-E is proven to moderate the relationship between anesthesiologists’ MF and WM in this study, interventions aimed at strengthening OS-E may help reduce MF and enhance WM, providing empirical justification for further research.

Based on these considerations, this study addressed the following research questions (RQs). RQ1. Is there a relationship between MF and intrinsic WM (IWM) among anesthesiologists? RQ2. Is there a relationship between MF and extrinsic WM (EWM) among anesthesiologists? RQ3. Is there a relationship between IWM and OS-E among anesthesiologists? RQ4. Is there a relationship between EWM and OS-E among anesthesiologists? RQ5. Is there a relationship between MF and OS-E among anesthesiologists? RQ6. Does OS-E moderate the relationship between MF and IWM among anesthesiologists? RQ7. Does OS-E moderate the relationship between MF and EWM among anesthesiologists?

The study utilizes explanatory correlational design from specifically employing moderated regression analysis to examine the regulatory role of OS-E in the relationship between MF and IWM/EWM [ 18 ].

In line with the objectives of the study, four measurement tools described below were administered to anesthesiologists.

The Personal Information Form included questions on demographic characteristics such as age and gender as well as factors defining the sample, including length of professional service and compulsory professional insurance status.

The MF Scale, developed by Katz et al. [ 19 ], consists of six items. It uses a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 ( strongly disagree ) to 5 ( strongly agree ) and is designed to assess physicians’ fear of malpractice. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Uğrak and Işık [ 20 ]. In present study, the Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient of the scale was 0.792.

The WM Scale was developed by Dündar et al. [ 21 ] and consists of two dimensions: IWM with nine items and EWM with 15 items. The scale uses a 5-point Likert-type format ranging from 1 ( strongly disagree ) to 5 ( strongly agree ) and measures employees’ WM. In this study, the Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient of the scale was 0.845 for the IWM dimension and 0.860 for the EWM dimension.

The OS-E Scale was developed by Schyns and von Collani [ 22 ] and adapted into Turkish by Çetin, Erenler-Tekmen, and Şentürk [ 23 ] in a study conducted among physicians. The scale comprises seven items rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 ( never ) to 5 ( always ). It measures individuals’ beliefs in their ability to perform behaviors required to achieve professional outcomes. In this study, the Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient of the scale was 0.866 for the OS-E.

As shown in Table 1 , the mean IWM score of the participating anesthesiologists was 33.31 ± 5.60, and the mean EWM score was 45.00 ± 9.58. The mean MF score was 25.10 ± 3.44. The mean OS-E score was 27.07 ± 3.90.

The sample size was calculated using the formula n = ( z × SD/d )² [ 18 ], which is applied when the population size is unknown. Among the measurement tools used, the WM scale adapted by Dündar et al. [ 21 ] had the highest standard deviation ( SD = 11.78). Therefore, this value was used to calculate the required sample size. At a 95% confidence level, the minimum sample size was calculated as n = (1.96 × 11.78/1.5)² = 237. Accordingly, 239 anesthesiologists were included in the study.

In this study, to reduce sampling bias, only participants who met the inclusion criteria for the study and volunteered to participate were allowed to join. To ensure sample diversity, the survey link was shared via various social media platforms, email groups, and professional networks. This was done to prevent data collection from being limited to a specific group or network. Indeed, the table presenting the descriptive data shows that the participants consisted of anesthesiologists working in different types of hospitals with different seniority levels. Participation was voluntary, and only individuals who answered “Yes” to the informed consent question were able to proceed with the survey. Convenience sampling was used.

Data were collected from 239 anesthesiologists working full-time in hospitals affiliated with the Ministry of Health in various provinces of Türkiye who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study and completed the study questionnaire administered via Google Forms. Data were collected between April and June 2024. All questionnaire items were marked as mandatory; therefore, there were no missing data, and responses from all participants were included in the analyses.

Data were evaluated using IBM SPSS Statistics Standard Concurrent User v26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics were reported as number of scale items (n), percentage (%), mean ( X ), standard deviation ( SD ), median ( M ), and minimum (min) and maximum (max) values. Normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis values; absolute skewness below ± 2.0 and kurtosis below 7.0 indicated normally distributed data [ 24 ]. In this study, the following values were noted: skewness 0.058, kurtosis − 0.160 for IWM; skewness 0.164, kurtosis 0.547 for EWM; skewness 0.508, kurtosis 0.102 for MF; and skewness 1.065, kurtosis 5.225 for OS-E. Based on these values, the data in this study were considered to exhibit normal distribution.

Pearson correlation coefficients were used to examine relationships among continuous variables. Moderation analyses were conducted using the regression-based path analysis model proposed by Hayes and Matthes [ 25 , 26 ]. The path analysis model was preferred as it can clarify the effects between variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. In the moderation models, interaction contribution (Δ R ²) and Cohen’s f ² coefficient were used to calculate the power of the model. Effect sizes were evaluated according to the criteria proposed by Cohen, with f ² values of 0.02, 0.15, and 0.35 indicating small, medium, and large effects, respectively [ 27 ].

The findings of the moderator role analysis indicate that the main effects of the MF and OS-E variables on both IWM ( R 2 = 0.165; F = 23.387; p < 0.001) and EWM ( R 2 = 0.245; F = 38.309; p < 0.001) in the general model are significant. Subsequently, adding the interaction term (MF*OS-E) to the model increases the total variance explained to 24.5% for IWM and 29.6% for EWM. The moderation analysis demonstrated that OS-E significantly moderated the relationship between MF and IWM ( β = −0.11, Δ R ² = 8%, F (3, 295) = 25.473, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.89, 1.33]). OS-E significantly moderated the relationship between MF and EWM ( β = −0.15, Δ R ² = 5.1%, F (3, 295) = 32.980, p < 0.001, 95% CI [− 0.17, − 0.05]).

In the moderation analyses, the effect size of the interaction term was evaluated using Cohen’s f ² coefficient. The effect size of the MF × OS-E interaction for IWM was small to moderate ( f ² = 0.106), whereas the interaction effect for EWM was small ( f ² = 0.072). When examining the Fig. 1 , it can be seen that for both the relationship between MF and IWM and the relationship between MF and EWM, the slope of the simple slope graph is steeper for anesthesiologists with high OS-E compared to anesthesiologists with low OS-E.

This study examined the moderating role of OS-E in the relationship between MF and WM among anesthesiologists. A key finding of the study demonstrated that MF was negatively associated with both IWM and EWM. In other words, as anesthesiologists’ MF increases, their levels of IWM and EWM decrease. High WM is critical in medical professions in terms of both employee performance and mental well-being [ 28 ]. Given its negative impact on the WM scores of anesthesiologists, MF should be considered a significant risk factor for physicians’ WM. Elevated MF may lead to behaviors such as avoidance (defensive behavior), excessive referrals for high-risk surgical procedures or intensive care admissions (negative defensive medicine behavior), or unnecessary diagnostic testing (positive defensive medicine behavior). Supporting this interpretation, Zuger [ 29 ] reported that physicians affected by malpractice lawsuits often adopt self-protective strategies, such as avoiding high-risk patients or avoiding medical specialties with high malpractice penalties. Liang [ 3 ] also identified MF as an important contributor to work stress and burnout among physicians. Although no previous studies have examined MF and WM specifically in anesthesiologists, Dırvar et al. [ 30 ] reported that MF negatively affected IWM among orthopedists. Taken together, these findings suggest that policy-level interventions aimed at reducing MF—such as malpractice risk awareness training, legal protections for physicians, and expanded insurance coverage—may help enhance physicians’ WM. In the literature review conducted by the authors, no previous study directly examining the relationship between MF and WM in anesthesiologists was found. Thus, the present study contributes to filling an important gap in literature.

The second major finding was that OS-E was positively associated with both IWM and EWM. This suggests that anesthesiologists who perceive themselves as professionally competent tend to have higher levels of IWM and EWM. In line with the present results, the literature has shown a positive relationship between OS-E and WM in different professional groups [ 31 – 33 ]. However, this study makes an important contribution to the literature as no study has examined this relationship among anesthesiologists.

The third key finding revealed a negative correlation between OS-E and MF, suggesting that anesthesiologists with higher OS-E tend to experience lower levels of fear related to medical errors. This finding aligns with the social cognitive theory, which emphasizes the role of self-efficacy beliefs in shaping individuals’ perceptions of stress and threat [ 12 ]. Anesthesiologists with high OS-E may perceive risks related to medical errors as more controllable, thereby experiencing lower levels of MF during patient care. Nevertheless, because this relationship is correlational, causal inferences cannot be made, and future experimental and longitudinal studies are needed to support these findings.

The most notable finding of the study is the determination that OS-E plays a regulatory role in both the relationship between MF and EWM and the relationship between MF and IWM. Graphical analyses unexpectedly show that MF’s negative effect on both IWM and EWM becomes more pronounced when OS-E is high. It means that a high OS-E amplifies the negative impact of MF on IWM and EWM. This finding may seem unexpected at first glance. However, this unexpected reinforcing effect can be explained by the fact that individuals with high self-efficacy have more perfectionist expectations of themselves and are more aware of their professional responsibilities and the consequences of their mistakes [ 34 ]. This finding, when considered together with the finding of a negative correlation between OS-E and MF, leads to the following interpretation. High OS-E in anesthesiologists may reduce MF levels, but when individuals with high OS-E encounter a situation that causes them to experience MF, the anxiety and fear they experience can have a more destructive effect on their WM. Furthermore, in individuals with high OS-E, MF may weaken WM by transforming it from an abstract concern into a concrete perception of risk that threatens professional identity and performance [ 35 ]. In this context, for individuals with high OS-E, MF may become a concrete risk perception that threatens professional identity, ethical stance, and professional competence, rather than an abstract and vague concern.

Although the interaction effects calculated for the regulatory model explain a statistically significant proportion of the variance, the R² change values (8% and 5.1%) indicate small effect sizes, suggesting that the practical effect of moderation should be interpreted carefully. The effect size of the MF × OS-E interaction for IWM was small to moderate, while the effect size for EWM was small [ 26 , 27 ]. From a clinical perspective, this finding suggests that the assumption that individuals with high OS-E are automatically at lower psychological risk may not be universally valid, and that this relationship holds limited significance for most individuals. The findings of this study demonstrate that OS-E does not consistently function as a protective factor in the relationship between MF and WM. Although the observed moderating effects were small-scale, the results show that higher levels of OS-E are associated with a stronger negative impact of MF on both IWM and EWM. This suggests that OS-E may function as a context-dependent reinforcing factor rather than a buffering mechanism. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting a nuanced and conditional process, highlighting the importance of considering numerous psychological and contextual variables when examining motivational outcomes in professional settings.

Despite its contributions, this study has certain limitations. First, data were collected online, restricting participation to anesthesiologists with internet access and limiting generalizability. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes causal inferences. Third, the study relied on self-report assessments. Although self-report methods are common in psychological research, relying on a single data source may inflate associations due to common method bias. Future studies using longitudinal, experimental, or multi-method designs may address these limitations. Additionally, the scope of the findings is constrained by the constructs examined in the measurement instruments used. This may have limited the representation of contextual or behavioral elements in the findings. Incorporating observational methods, semi-structured interviews, experimental designs, or mixed-method approaches could provide a more comprehensive understanding. Finally, the sample consisted solely of Turkish anesthesiologists, limiting the generalizability of the findings to other specialties and cultural contexts. Future studies should examine MF, WF, and O-SE in other medical specialties and across different cultures.

In light of the study findings and relevant theoretical frameworks, OS-E does not function as a uniform protective resource in the relationship between MF and WM. Conversely, in physicians working in medical fields requiring high professional responsibility, such as anesthesiology and resuscitation, higher OS-E may increase individuals’ susceptibility to MF and thus increase motivation risks. Based on these findings, it may be useful to examine the interaction between negative motivational outcomes associated with MF in high-responsibility healthcare fields such as anesthesiology and external factors such as organizational factors and working conditions, in addition to psychological factors related to employees.
