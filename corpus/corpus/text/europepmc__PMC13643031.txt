The underutilization of immigrant skills costs the Canadian economy $50 billion annually (Agopsowicz and Billy‐Ochien 2019 ). Despite being admitted to Canada as “the best and the brightest” and holding permanent residency status, immigrants face underemployment, low wages, and poverty. While many studies address the challenges of immigrant labour market integration, they often focus on the immigrant perspective, with few examining the role of local policy actors, such as human resource management (HRM) professionals, who make and influence hiring decisions. Hiring professionals play a crucial role as gatekeepers in the labour market, determining who gains access to employment opportunities. These include HRM professionals (i.e., HR recruiters, advisors, consultants, and managers, external recruiters, and hiring panels) and hiring managers (line or team supervisors). Using interviews with HRM professionals, this paper focuses on the factors deemed most important by HRM professionals for immigrants’ employment eligibility in Alberta, Canada. The study finds that during the hiring process, HRM professionals often define and assess immigrants, particularly non‐White 1 immigrants, as temporary migrants. This misclassification has significant negative consequences. Markers of perceived temporary foreign worker status, such as foreign names, accents, credentials, and being non‐White, trigger heightened scrutiny. HRM professionals question immigrants’ legal work eligibility, language proficiency, relevant experience, and credential equivalency, framing these as administrative burdens or risks. To synthesize these dynamics, the study develops a typology of six overlapping categorical distinctions through which immigrant applicants are evaluated during hiring. These distinctions capture how immigrants are variously constructed as temporary workers, administrative burdens, culturally incompatible, risks or threats, foreign‐trained ideologues, and racial‐cultural others. While analytically distinct, these categories operate relationally to limit immigrants’ claims to employment. These findings highlight a disconnect between government policies that grant immigrants privileged preference in hiring within the Canadian labour market and HRM professionals’ perceptions and hiring practices.

The study employs Relational Inequality Theory (RIT) to examine immigrant employment as a claim‐making process. Tomaskovic‐Devey and Avent‐Holt ( 2019 ) argue that to understand how social inequality and immigrant exclusion are produced in the Canadian labour market, we must focus on the relations between people, positions, and organizations. Social actors within organizations develop and enact hierarchies of power and status through categorical distinctions between roles, jobs, and people. Categorical distinctions based on race, skill (level and type), immigration status, class, and gender are assigned value and used to allocate rewards and justify exclusion. RIT explains immigrant exclusion through three mechanisms: exploitation, social closure, and claim‐making. Exploitation involves more powerful actors appropriating resources such as promotions and jobs from categorically distinct and less powerful groups, often justifying this practice by assigning them a lower status. For example, immigrants, despite being qualified for employment, are often precariously employed and paid less for work performed by citizens (Shan 2013 ). Social closure refers to gatekeeping and opportunity hoarding, where powerful actors distribute resources only to ingroup members who share similar characteristics such as race, gender, citizenship, or professional status. Claim‐making is the “discursive articulation of why one actor is more deserving of organizational resources than others” (Tomaskovic‐Devey and Avent‐Holt 2019 , 163). The process involves one or more actors putting forward a claim to organizational resources. That claim is then either accepted as legitimate or dismissed by influential others. Both who can make claims and which claims are deemed legitimate are shaped by relations of status and power, including perceived competence, individual and collective power, and actors’ structural positions within those relations. Claim‐making is relational and political because it occurs through interactions among actors situated in unequal power relations and entails struggles over resources and authority, thereby activating exploitation and social closure (Tomaskovic‐Devey and Avent‐Holt 2019 , 164). Immigrants, like other job applicants, make claims to HRM professionals about why they deserve to be hired. RIT also emphasizes that categorical distinctions are shaped by local hiring cultures connected to broader societal fields such as human resource management, immigration, accreditation, and employment and immigration law. These fields embed ideologies and rationales into organizational culture and workplace practices, often normalizing exploitation, social closure, and exclusion. RIT raises several questions explored in this study: What does the categorical distinction “immigrant” mean within hiring processes? What is the relationship between immigrants and organizational hiring actors (HRM professionals, hiring managers, and hiring panels)? How do intersecting social categories, such as skill, race, class, gender, culture, and religion, shape immigrants' evaluation during hiring and access to jobs?

In Canada, research consistently shows that immigrants, particularly newcomers and non‐White immigrants, experience earning differentials, higher poverty rates, and underemployment compared to Canadian‐born workers (Hou and Picot 2024 ; Lightman and Gingrich 2018 ; Liu 2019 ; Reitz et al. 2014 ). Agopsowicz and Billy‐Ochieng ( 2019 ) reported that the median earnings gap between immigrants and Canadian‐born workers widened from 3.8% in 1986 to 10.3% in 2018, affecting both immigrant women and men across most occupations except the sciences. Despite immigrants' high levels of education, Statistics Canada (Hou et al. 2019 ) reports that only one‐half were matched in jobs requiring university degrees. Primarily, employers in Canada only hired immigrants in low‐ and medium‐skilled jobs (Agopsowicz and Billy‐Ochieng 2019 ; Picot and Lu 2017 ). Statistics Canada has pointed to recent improvements in immigrant economic outcomes as evidence of strengthened labour market integration (Hou 2024 ). While aggregate data show rising employment rates and earnings among recent immigrants through 2022–2023, Statistics Canada reports that the earnings gap between immigrants and Canadian‐born workers persists (Hou 2024 ). In 2022, 36% of admitted immigrants were “two‐step” migrants with prior Canadian work experience through temporary programs, and these individuals tend to experience stronger post‐landing earnings growth than “one‐step” immigrants admitted directly from abroad (Hou 2024 ). Yet stronger relative performance among two‐step migrants does not eliminate broader disparities. Although income gaps have narrowed in recent years, immigrants, regardless of steps, continue to earn less, on average, than Canadian‐born citizens. Moreover, the median entry wage of new immigrants declined in 2023 compared to the previous year, despite remaining above pre‐pandemic levels (Statistics Canada 2025 ). These trends suggest that aggregate improvements in immigrant earnings and employment are limited to two‐step migrants and coexist with persistent structural inequalities in immigrant labour market outcomes (Hou 2024 ; Statistics Canada 2025 ).

Beyond immigration status, race and gender disparities in labour market outcomes are particularly pronounced. Non‐White immigrants face higher unemployment rates and lower earnings compared to White immigrants and Canadian‐born workers (Guo 2015 ; Lightman and Gingrich 2018 ). Yssaad and Fields ( 2018 ) finds that White immigrants experience outcomes similar to Canadian‐born workers, while non‐White immigrants continue to face exclusion. Gingrich et al. ( 2023 ) similarly report that White men, whether Canadian‐born or immigrant, face the least economic exclusion. In contrast, non‐White immigrants, despite having higher educational attainment, remain underemployed and are blocked from accessing high‐status, high‐skilled, and high‐paying jobs (Lightman and Gingrich 2018 ; Reitz et al. 2014 ). Immigrant women face persistent challenges in securing employment, earning lower average entry wages than male principal applicants (Elrick and Lightman 2016 ; Hussen 2018 ; Yssaad and Fields 2018 ). Hou et al. ( 2021 ) finds that the employment rate gap between new immigrant women and Canadian‐born women remained largely unchanged from 2001 to 2019. Non‐White immigrant women experience the most significant labour market disadvantages and the highest levels of economic exclusion (Gingrich et al. 2023 ; Liu 2019 ). This is the case even within immigrant occupational niches such as healthcare and teaching, where non‐White immigrant women often occupy the bottom rung of the occupational ladder (Creese and Wiebe 2012 ; Thomas and Lightman 2022 ; Wijesingha and Ramos 2017 ). These reports highlight that race and gender shape immigrant employment, job quality and labour market outcomes.

Hiring occurs within a legal environment governed by federal and provincial regulations. Human rights legislation, such as the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and the Alberta Human Rights Act, prohibits discrimination in hiring unless it is based on a bona fide occupational requirement, 2 and it requires employers to accommodate employees and job applicants up to the point of undue hardship (Alberta Human Rights Commission 2021 ).

Canadian labour and employment laws prioritize Canadian citizens and permanent residents. Until 2021, the Public Service Employment Act gave first preference to citizens over permanent residents (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat 2021 ). Similarly, federal programs like the Temporary Foreign Worker Program (TFWP) require employers to demonstrate efforts to hire citizens and permanent residents before considering foreign workers (Foster and Barnetson 2017 ; Government of Canada 2024a , 2024b ). Government‐funded employment assistance programs further reinforce this preference. Outside of government settings, employers maintain hiring hierarchies by favoring Canadian credentials and experience, advantages typically gained through longer residence or citizenship. Provincial regulatory bodies also prioritize Canadian citizens through licensing and trade requirements (Fairness for Newcomers Office 2024 ; Reitz et al. 2014 ). In practice, many licensing frameworks are structured around domestic training pathways, labour mobility agreements, and documentation requirements that align more directly with Canadian educational and professional systems (Brosseau 2021 ; Reitz et al. 2014 ). Although immigrants are legally eligible to obtain licensure, these institutional arrangements, such as supervised Canadian work experience, often position Canadian citizens, those trained in Canada and those from Western countries to meet requirements more quickly and with fewer procedural barriers (Picot and Hou 2019 ; Zhang et al. 2023 ). Immigrants with foreign training often face multi‐stage credential assessments, examinations, and requirements for supervised Canadian work experience, which can delay entry into regulated occupations and contribute to occupational displacement. Employers are hesitant to hire immigrants, particularly internationally trained immigrants within regulated professions (Brosseau 2021 ; Zhang et al. 2023 ).

Organizational structures also shape hiring practices. Strategic Human Resource Management (SHRM), as a professional logic, aligns HRM functions and hiring strategies with organizational goals such as profitability, efficiency, and risk avoidance (Gill 2018 ; Lepak and Shaw 2008 ). SHRM encourages HRM professionals to act as business partners supporting hiring managers, who hold line authority over hiring decisions, while HRM professionals generally have advisory or staff authority (Dessler and Chhinzer 2017 ). As a result, hiring managers often exercise greater decision‐making power. Also, SHRM promotes the view that HRM professionals see hiring managers and hiring panels as internal clients and customers. This relationship is more pronounced for external HRM professionals at staffing and recruiting firms seeking repeat business.

Alberta's hiring culture reinforces a Canada‐first orientation, privileging citizens and then permanent residents in employment (Foster and Barnetson 2015 ). A common practice among Canadian organizations is that job posts often state that preference is given to Canadian citizens and permanent residents when hiring (Johnston 2019 ). As a province, Alberta has demonstrated its preference for citizens via successive internal migrant labour programs such as Alberta is Calling (Hiller 2009 ). Historically and presently, many employers continue to favor White Canadian‐born workers and express doubts about the human capital competency and the ability of non‐White immigrants to adapt to the ideals and values of White Western Canadian society (Guo 2015 ; Foster and Barnetson 2017 ; Intungane et al. 2024 ).

Although researchers frequently study immigrant labour market outcomes, there is less focus on employers' roles, particularly HRM professionals. As Zikic ( 2015 ) notes, despite skilled immigrants being a vital labour source, management scholars have paid scant attention to their value to organizations and best practices for attracting and integrating them. Hiring practices are often not evidence‐based. Risavy et al. ( 2021 ) found that Canadian IT firms rely heavily on subjective assessments, resumes, cover letters, and social media profiles, rather than validated tools like skills assessments or emotional intelligence tests. This aligns with Mann and Chowhan ( 2011 ), who found that Canadian firms rarely adopt evidence‐based HRM selection practices such as job knowledge tests, shown to benefit workers and organizational productivity. Rivera ( 2015 ) highlights that this is often the case, as employers often prioritize likeability and shared group membership over productivity. Rivera ( 2015 ) concludes that a greater focus should be on how employers make hiring decisions in practice and how emotions mediate such processes.

Only a few Canadian studies directly examine HRM professionals' role in immigrant labour market integration. Oreopoulos ( 2011 ) conducted an audit study, sending over 13,000 resumes to employers in Toronto and Montreal. He found substantial discrimination against applicants with foreign experience or non‐English names and noted that listing high‐status credentials did little to reduce discrimination or improve callback. Although Oreopoulos' follow‐up interviews had a low response rate, his findings reveal discriminatory hiring practices, highlighting the need to examine the organizational processes enabling discrimination. Sakamoto et al. ( 2010 ) explores the lack of “Canadian experience” as an employment rationale, finding no consistent definition and suggesting the responsibility of integration falls unfairly on immigrants. Elrick and Lightman ( 2016 ) adds an organizational perspective through interviews with 20 ICT hiring managers, showing that definitions of “skills,” emphasis on “cultural fit,” and reliance on internal networks shape immigrant hiring outcomes. Her study underscores the role of social and cultural capital in shaping immigrant employability evaluations.

Overall, the literature shows that immigrants, particularly non‐White groups and women, fare worse in the Canadian labour market. Most research uses quantitative methods like regression analyses on labour market surveys, while employer‐focused studies rely on resume audits and qualitative interviews. There is a clear need for more qualitative research into hiring practices, organizational contexts, and how intersectional identities affect immigrant competency evaluation and labour market integration. Given that hiring processes involve HRM professionals, hiring managers (supervisors), and panels (committees), this study uses the term “hiring professionals” to refer to all three groups. Alberta is a useful study site because it has one of the worst immigrant earnings gaps in Canada, with immigrants’ hourly wages significantly lower regardless of educational attainment (Agopsowicz and Billy‐Ochien 2019 ). Yet, the province is experiencing rapid population growth driven largely by international migration (Statistics Canada 2024 ), and Calgary is the third most common destination for immigrants to Canada (Statistics Canada 2025b ). The study allows us to see the organizational processes and considerations that shape HRM professionals’ evaluations of immigrant job applicants in a Canadian province where immigration plays an important role in meeting workforce needs.

This study employed a qualitative methodology using thirty‐one in‐depth interviews with HRM professionals in Alberta, Canada, conducted by the first author via Zoom between 2022 and 2023. Participants included HRM managers, advisors, talent acquisition consultants, recruiters, and external consultants involved in resume screening, interviewing, and hiring. Participants were recruited through the Chartered Professionals in Human Resources of Alberta (CPHR Alberta), a non‐profit HRM professional association with over 6300 members (Beauchamp 2024 ). Recruitment flyers were shared via CPHR Alberta's communications and website. Inclusion criteria required participants to be involved in employee recruitment, selection, and/or serve on a hiring panel. The interview guide was designed as a comparative, semi‐structured instrument examining how decisions unfold across stages of hiring and whether these processes shift when an applicant is identified as an immigrant. The first set of questions mapped the standard hiring workflow, including evaluation criteria, resume screening, interview selection, advancement thresholds, and credential verification. The second set mirrored these questions but focused specifically on immigrant applicants, enabling systematic comparison within the same interview.

Participants self‐identified their demographic information, including gender, race, and hiring experience, as well as organizational context (size, sector, government contracts). Of the 31 participants, 21 identified as female and 10 as male; 16 identified as White and 15 as racialized (10 Asian, 4 Black, 1 Latino). Participants represented various sectors: Oil and Gas (6), Postsecondary Institutions (5), Information Technology (3), Government (3), Accounting (3), Utilities (2), Construction (2), Manufacturing (2), Services (2), Healthcare (1), Food Retail (1), and Non‐profit (1). There were 26 internal HRM professionals and five external recruiters. Most worked as internal HRM professionals across small (5), medium (13), and large firms (13), with a median of 10 years of hiring experience. Interviews lasted between 60 and 90 min.

The research question guiding the study is: What factors do HRM professionals consider most important for immigrant employment eligibility? To investigate this, the first author asked questions such as: What is most important when deciding whether an immigrant advances in the hiring process? Does it matter if their credentials and work experience are Canadian? If so, why? How do you assess whether to invite an immigrant for an interview? The semi‐structured, open‐ended nature of the interviews allowed participants to expand beyond these prompts. For example, questions like “What happens when an immigrant submits a resume and cover letter?” sparked discussions about how immigrants are defined, and which factors influence their evaluation. These conversations surfaced unexpected findings which are often overlooked in existing Canadian literature. Participants also reflected on organizational dynamics, including the roles of multiple actors, competing interests, and internal decision‐making processes.

All interviews were transcribed verbatim, de‐identified, and uploaded to NVivo for analysis. Transcripts were systematically coded through four iterative coding cycles, drawing on interview content, analytical memos, and theoretical frameworks in Canadian immigration and hiring research (Creswell 2013 ; Saldana 2013 ). We went through four coding cycles. In the first cycle, we coded broadly for the main topics discussed, including recruitment strategies, screening immigrants, and selection criteria. In the second cycle, we refined the codes and grouped related codes into categories. For example, “Canadian experience” was initially a broad code and was later separated into Canadian work experience, Canadian credentials, and Canadian cultural signals. The third cycle focused on research questions and used process and values coding to examine how HRM professionals evaluated immigrant applicants, defined immigrants, and the factors they considered in hiring (Saldaña 2013 ). Finally, we focused on thematic coding by looking for patterns among categories, the prevalence and significance of practices or processes, and the relationships between coding categories. Themes such as administrative burden, risk management, and financial cost emerged from the analysis of coding categories and participant language (e.g., mentions of “risk,” “ease,” or “quick”). We noted the various aspects of each theme and then reviewed each subsequent transcript for any new aspects, dimensions, or nuances, continuing this process until no new information emerged. We developed themes iteratively through recoding, refining categories, analytic memos, paying attention to context, and consideration of the theoretical framework and relevant literature. We then discussed, compared, and resolved differences in coding and interpretation through consensus. To address the question, “What factors do HRM professionals view as most important for immigrant hiring eligibility in Alberta?” frequency analyses of participant responses to key questions were conducted to examine how factors such as language, credentials, and perceived risk were discussed. We examined divergent accounts and differences among participants to refine the emerging categories and themes. For example, although “immigrant risk” appeared across participants’ accounts, assessments of this risk differed by company size. We examined these differences to determine whether they reflected exceptions or variations within a category or theme. We assessed saturation by examining the recurrence of patterns across participants’ accounts and comparing codes, categories, and themes across interviews to determine whether new patterns continued to emerge. Typologies were created during coding; we identified recurring ways HRM professionals described immigrant applicants and grouped them into broader categories. We refined these categories by comparing them across the interviews. RIT guided our interpretation of these distinctions as categorical processes through which organizational actors construct the “immigrant job applicant.”

These responses indicate that HRM professionals in our sample equate immigrants with temporary migrants rather than permanent residents, invoking categories such as refugees, individuals with limited language proficiency, holders of foreign credentials, or applicants on temporary work visas. These distinctions emerge during the recruitment and selection process and shape how applicants are evaluated. From a RIT perspective, this pattern reflects misrecognition, as immigrant job applicants who are legal permanent residents are treated as presumptively temporary, requiring some to prove their legal right to work before their qualifications are assessed. RIT emphasizes that these types of categorical distinctions help produce and maintain organizational hierarchies by operating as mechanisms of social closure. By reinforcing assumptions of temporariness and otherness, these classifications constrain immigrants’ ability to claim equal access to employment during hiring. These categorical distinctions and definitions of immigrants as temporary migrant workers shape how immigrant applicants are evaluated and the factors deemed most important for immigrant employment eligibility in Alberta, Canada.

Interviews with HRM professionals in this study reveal that immigrants are systematically disadvantaged throughout the hiring process. Recurring themes and categorical distinctions among HRM professionals include the immigrant as a “Temporary Worker,” an “Administrative Burden,” and “Culturally Incompatible.” These perceptions form persistent distinctions in hiring, framing immigrants as organizational burdens requiring greater financial cost, time, training, and adjustment than Canadian‐born candidates. These distinctions shape job applicant claim‐making by defining only citizens as deserving of employment and requiring immigrants to overcome presumptions of temporality, cost, and cultural incompatibility. As a result, immigrants’ claims to organizational resources are weakened from the outset and made contingent on demonstrating extraordinary compensatory value, thereby creating unequal standards of evaluation and reinforcing social closure.

Another factor disadvantaging immigrant job applicants is the challenge HRM professionals perceive in evaluating educational quality and equivalency. Most HRM professionals in our sample noted that immigrant education was often perceived as inferior or difficult to assess during applicant screening. Winnie, an HR advisor, explained that hiring professionals often perceive evaluating immigrant credentials as burdensome, which discourages them from considering immigrant applicants: If you're coming from another country, maybe their educational, whatever, university degrees, or professional associations, requirements that they have there might not be as equivalent here. So maybe they have to do upgrading. Or maybe it's just a lot of extra work to basically kind of be considered at the same equivalent level (Winnie, HR Advisor, Postsecondary).

These explanations reflected a tendency of hiring professionals to treat foreign credentials as less trustworthy or of lower quality. Many HRM professionals in our sample often rely on assumptions and stereotypes about countries' educational systems and racial stereotypes, limiting immigrants’ ability to make claims based on their qualifications.

Overall, perceptions of educational equivalency reinforce the view of immigrants as human capital‐deficient workers, making them less desirable hires. This perception fuels hiring practices that privilege Canadian‐educated candidates and limit immigrants’ employment regardless of actual competence. Most HRM professionals are unaware of or do not use the educational credential assessments (ECAs) service offered by the government, the International Qualifications Assessment Service (IQAS), 4 which is held by 58.4% of immigrants and could help address credential equivalency and reduce financial and administrative concerns (Miller 2023 ). Only four out of the 31 HRM professionals interviewed were familiar with IQAS, and only those working at the provincial government (3) utilize IQAS or ask job applicants to submit language and educational credential assessments if they are internationally trained.

English language proficiency is equally important in determining immigrant employment eligibility. All HRM professionals in our study shared that hiring professionals often categorized immigrants as lacking English language proficiency before and during applicant screening, which prevents them from being selected. These assumptions serve as mechanisms of social closure, requiring immigrants to prove their language ability in ways not expected of Canadian‐born applicants. Courtney, a talent acquisition advisor, observed: There's often an assumption in the organization that immigrants have language barriers, that they won't be able to communicate well with, you know, patients or other staff members. That's simply not true, in my experience (Courtney, Talent Acquisition Advisor, Government).

This means that a job candidate deemed an immigrant is assessed for English proficiency and the ability to speak with a Canadian rather than a foreign accent. Like educational credentials assessments, most HRM professionals and hiring managers do not use language proficiency assessments. Only four out of the 31 HRM professionals interviewed were familiar with language proficiency assessments. Consequently, most HRM professionals in our sample relied on subjective language assessments such as Canadian accents, errors on application documents, and assumptions about immigrants’ lack of English proficiency, collectively denying immigrant employment claims for consideration. In contrast, similar issues among Canadian‐born applicants are often overlooked, thereby bolstering citizens' employment prospects. Overall, language proficiency serves as a mechanism of social closure in immigrant employment.

The selection of immigrants for jobs is often contingent upon their perceived cultural fit. As defined by most HRM professionals in the study, cultural fit is an applicant's perceived ability to align with White Canadian and company‐specific workplace norms, values, and behaviors. These assessments are deeply shaped by assumptions about race, religion, education, language, and social conformity. Cultural fit is frequently framed during the evaluation of immigrant candidates as a necessity, an indicator of their suitability for jobs, and a question of administrative burden and financial risks. Sam, a recruiter, explained: So oftentimes, the work that it takes to, I'm gonna use a terrible word, which I hate using this word, “assimilate.” If they're not able to assimilate easily, or they haven't already been accustomed to the North American culture or Western culture, they're going to have difficulties. And hiring managers will not have the time and the resources, generally speaking, to then either train or have patience for, or educate, or coach, or mentor. They prefer candidates who are already at that stage (Sam, Recruiter, Postsecondary).

Together, these accounts show that cultural fit, though framed in terms of risk, efficiency, ease, or team compatibility, operates as a mechanism of racialized social closure. Cultural fit assessments impose additional requirements on non‐White immigrant applicants to demonstrate belonging through Canadian experience, credential recognition, cultural references, accents, ideological values, and even personal adjustments such as name changes. Within this logic, “risk” functions as a coded rationale that recasts racialized cultural difference as potential organizational liability, transforming exclusion into seemingly prudent risk management rather than discrimination. HRM professionals frame risk in economic and relational terms, invoking concerns about productivity, credential validity, client perceptions, and workplace cohesion, yet these judgments are socially produced within unequal power relations rather than evaluations of competence.

While Canadian immigration policies are designed to select immigrants from middle‐class backgrounds (Elrick 2021 ) with high levels of human capital, including advanced degrees and substantial professional experience, these credentials and prior class positions are often not recognized by HRM professionals as markers of professional status. Instead, candidates are constructed as culturally and human capital deficient based on their country of origin and training institutions. References to familiarity with the Stampede, the CN Tower, or participation in ice hockey function less as neutral indicators of middle‐class belonging and more as racialized tests of conformity to locally dominant White professional norms, reinforcing middle‐class boundaries within Alberta. These expectations are unidirectional, requiring no reciprocal adaptation from hiring professionals and legitimating exclusion under the guise of SHRM neutrality and economic rationality. Rather than assessing candidates’ knowledge, skills, and abilities through standardized tools, hiring decisions are shaped by cultural matching and informal evaluations.

Applying for employment is a process of claim‐making. Through job applications, applicants articulate their skills, work experience, and qualifications to assert their legitimacy and claim access to organizational resources. Yet non‐White immigrant job applicants encounter multiple categorical distinctions during hiring that reshape how their claims are evaluated and restrict their access to equal consideration.

The legitimacy of these employment claims is structured, in part, by legal status. In Canada, the federal and provincial governments distinguish between temporary migrants and permanent immigrants in their legal and policy definitions. Under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (IRPA), immigrants are defined as permanent residents with the right to live, work, or study anywhere in Canada and are protected under Canadian law and the Charter of Rights and Freedoms (Government of Canada 2023 ). In contrast, temporary migrants are foreign nationals without the rights associated with permanent residency (Government of Canada 2023 ). To address employment barriers, the federal government amended the Public Service Employment Act to ensure equal consideration for citizens and permanent residents in public sector hiring (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat 2021 ). Interviews with HRM professionals in our sample reveal that these legal distinctions often collapse in practice. Immigrants, particularly non‐White immigrants, were repeatedly misrecognized as temporary migrants rather than as legally entitled and preferred workers. As a result, their claims to preference in hiring and their rights to work may be disregarded as they are evaluated as though they are temporary foreign applicants. This misrecognition emerges as an additional and underexamined mechanism shaping poor labour market outcomes, alongside widely discussed issues such as skills mismatch and credential devaluation.

Across the interviews, HRM participants cited administrative burden, lack of trust, financial costs, and sponsorship requirements as reasons not to hire immigrants. Yet these concerns are misplaced, since immigrants as permanent residents do not require work permits or sponsorship and are obligated to reside in Canada under IRPA's residency requirements (Legislative Services Branch 2023 ). Trained within SHRM frameworks that emphasize profitability, efficiency, and risk avoidance, HRM professionals interpret immigrant candidates, particularly non‐White immigrants, as posing challenges to these business objectives. The “Immigrant‐as‐Administrative‐Burden” operates as a risk‐based categorical classification that assigns immigrant applicants to a high‐risk category regardless of their legal status. References to documentation complexity, relocation and training costs, and longer hiring timelines position immigrants as unstable and costly, with perceived temporariness serving as a proxy for future risk.

This categorization functions as a mechanism of claim delegitimization. Rather than assessing candidates based on knowledge, skills, and abilities, HRM professionals in our study evaluate them through perceived cost and instability, weakening immigrant claims before formal assessments of competency. These evaluations are intertwined with racialized and culturalized assumptions: credentials from non‐White countries are interpreted as signaling weaker language proficiency or lower skill equivalency, and cultural difference is framed as a threat to team cohesion. SHRM rationality provides a seemingly neutral vocabulary for exclusion, yet the standards used to assess risk are themselves shaped by racialized cultural assumptions, enabling social closure under the guise of organizational efficiency.

Immigrant exclusion also begins at the earliest stages of the hiring process. HRM professionals in our sample, despite their advisory role, were often unfamiliar with immigration programs under Express Entry, the primary pathway through which most skilled immigrants enter Canada and a major labour source for employers (Miller 2023). Their lack of knowledge meant they could not correct misconceptions when hiring managers raised questions about immigrant employment eligibility, language proficiency, or Canadian education equivalency. Most HRM professionals in our study also ignored government‐issued credential and language assessments, resulting in instances where immigrant applicants were screened out before reaching the interview stage, where they could have clarified their eligibility and skills. The limited use of formal credential assessment mechanisms weakens immigrant employment claim‐making by removing standardized pathways for recognizing qualifications as legitimate and equivalent to those of Canadian‐born applicants. Without structured evaluation tools such as language and credential assessments, foreign credentials are judged through subjective and racialized assumptions, raising the bar for immigrants to be seen as qualified.

These findings extend Canadian debates on the credential recognition problem by showing that recognition issues encompass more than foreign education or experience; they also involve immigrants’ legal status. Previous research has highlighted how non‐White immigrants’ professional qualifications are undervalued (Banerjee et al. 2018 , 2025 ; Oreopoulos 2011 ). The study extends Oreopoulos’ ( 2011 ) findings on name discrimination by showing that exclusion occurs long before résumé callbacks. Instead of being judged on work experience or educational competency, non‐White immigrants in our sample were at times excluded based on racial and cultural differences before their qualifications were even considered.

The study also illustrates how cultural racism operates as a key relational mechanism. Cultural racism involves misrepresenting and devaluing the practices, values, and expressions of non‐White immigrants, positioning them as incompatible with Canadian norms (Alcoff 2023 ; Triadafilopoulos 2013 ; Wiseman 2018 ). White cultural values, often coded as Canadian or Western, were constructed as normative, while immigrant cultural practices were depicted as backward, deficient, or threatening. Non‐White immigrants’ cultures were portrayed as irreconcilable with Western Canadian values, which was then used to question their rights to employment and equal treatment. Even HRM professionals who lead diversity initiatives often encouraged immigrants to “whiten” their résumés by including Canadian credentials, volunteering, or changing their names (Kang et al. 2016 ). Yet interviews revealed that discomfort with managing non‐White workers still produced exclusion, underscoring that cultural fit is not neutral but racialized. Ultimately, cultural deficiency was used to justify immigrant exclusion without explicitly naming race.

This study contributes to Canadian immigration scholarship by identifying how HRM professionals rely on six distinct yet overlapping relational categorical distinctions and logics to justify immigrant exclusion: (1) “Temporary Workers,” (2) “Administrative Burdens,” (3) “Culturally Incompatible,” (4) “Risks and Threats,” (5) “Foreign‐Trained Ideologues,” and (6) the “Racial‐Cultural Other.” The typology developed in this study further advances scholarship on racial discrimination in Canada by identifying how exclusion is enacted through overlapping categorical distinctions such as the “Culturally Incompatible” or the “Racial‐Cultural Other.” These mechanisms illustrate how cultural racism translates into employment inequality. The study also extends Rivera's ( 2015 ) work on cultural matching by showing that in Canada, matching is racialized: immigrants are expected to align with White Canadian norms rather than simply share educational or social backgrounds. Ultimately, by highlighting the disconnect between immigration policy and organizational practice, this research underscores the need for reforms that target employers’ knowledge and practices, rather than just immigrant training or bridge programs.

Theoretically, this study extends Relational Inequality Theory (RIT) by showing how legal categories and cultural schemas intersect to produce immigrant exclusion in organizations (Tomaskovic‐Devey and Avent‐Holt 2019 ). While RIT has traditionally been applied to examine inequality among employees within organizations, this study demonstrates its value for analyzing how HRM policies and practices affect immigrants before they even enter the workplace. The study extends the theory beyond internal workplace dynamics to capture how organizational actors, particularly HRM professionals, interpret legal categories, mobilize cultural schemas, and distribute employment opportunities. It demonstrates, first, how immigration status categories, alongside race and culture, function as relational boundaries that shape access to employment. HRM professionals’ failure to recognize immigrants as permanent residents shows how organizational actors reinterpret and distort legal categories, undermining the intent of Canadian immigration policy. Second, it illustrates how cultural racism operates within hiring processes to legitimate inequality. By situating immigrants’ exclusion within RIT, the study highlights how categorical distinctions such as “temporary foreign worker” or “culturally incompatible” operate as relational boundaries that deny non‐White immigrants equal access to jobs. In doing so, it introduces a novel lens for understanding how inequality is produced through hiring processes and employer engagement. This application broadens the theory's scope within Canadian immigration scholarship but underscores its potential to illuminate how organizational practices reproduce, or could mitigate, systemic exclusion.

A limitation of the study is its recruitment strategy and geographic scope. Participants were recruited solely through the largest HRM professional organization, CPHR Alberta, so not all HRM professionals in the province had an equal opportunity to participate. The sample also did not include hiring managers, such as team managers and supervisors. The study is based on interviews with HRM professionals and therefore provides their perspectives on hiring managers, hiring panels, and organizational practices rather than direct accounts from these other actors. Also, the findings are limited to the specific context of Alberta and may not be generalizable to other provinces. Social desirability bias is another limitation of the study (Bergen and Labonté 2020 ). Participants may have been reluctant to discuss hiring practices openly given the perceived risks of disclosure and may have attributed responsibility for some actions to hiring managers. To mitigate this, participants were assured that pseudonyms would be used in interview transcripts and that identifying information, such as company names, would be removed. Nevertheless, the study provides detailed insight into how HRM professionals in Alberta understand and evaluate immigrant job candidates, as well as the policies and practices they observed while serving on hiring panels. Another strength is the inclusion of HRM professionals from major employers in Alberta. More research is needed to assess whether similar practices occur in other provinces and to examine the perspectives of hiring managers and other panel members.

The policy implications are significant. HRM professionals function as labour market gatekeepers who are actively involved in immigrant management within organizations, acting as an extension of the immigration regime. Given the role of hiring professionals in deciding which applicants are recruited, shortlisted, and ultimately hired, they exercise discretionary authority that shapes immigrant labour market integration and overall outcomes. In doing so, they determine for whom the benefits of permanent residency accrue. As a result, Canada's goal of attracting highly skilled immigrants/permanent residents is weakened at the organizational level, where hiring practices reassess the value of immigrants’ status and credentials. If permanent residents, legally entitled and preferred workers under Canadian immigration law, do not receive recognition as such in hiring, the credibility of Canada's immigration system is undermined. Current investments in language testing, credential recognition, and bridge programs will remain insufficient if employers and HRM professionals fail to incorporate these assessments into hiring. Addressing immigrant exclusion requires stronger enforcement of human rights protections and targeted measures to improve employer knowledge of immigration processes (Ontario Human Rights Commission 2013 ). Initiatives such as self‐directed micro‐credentials for HRM professionals and hiring managers, covering Express Entry eligibility, credential and language assessments, and the Comprehensive Ranking System (CRS) framework, could help align hiring practices with immigration policy goals. Ultimately, the study suggests that legal eligibility to work is not enough when hiring professionals disregard immigrants’ permanent residency status and rely on racialized and cultural logics of exclusion. By bridging the gap between immigration policy's promise of high‐skilled integration and the persistent poor outcomes of non‐White immigrants, this research highlights how relational inequality is enacted in everyday hiring practices. More serious consideration must be given to immigrants not only as newcomers with skills to be integrated but as legally entitled and preferred workers whose rights and contributions are systematically denied in Canada's labour market.
