Burnout, a condition that encompasses mental exhaustion, negativity, and lower productivity, has adverse consequences for individuals, employers, and society at large. Burnout can affect not only a worker's mental health but also their job efficacy and tenure, which leads to negative organizational consequences such as weakened cohesion, accidents, and costly turnovers. Burnout is an extended response to persistent stressors in the workplace and can result in overwhelming exhaustion, feelings of cynicism and detachment from the job, and a sense of ineffectiveness and failure [1] . Workers are empowered when they perceive that their work environments provide opportunity for growth and access to power needed to carry out job demands [2] . When these conditions are lacking, employees feel powerlessness. This threatens organizational productivity since powerless individuals are more susceptible to burnout and reduced job satisfaction [3] .

The repercussions can be particularly harmful in the case of the healthcare sector. Burned-out and stressed healthcare practitioners may be more prone to making mistakes and providing sub-optimal care, which could harm their patients [4] , [5] . Moreover, to the extent that burned-out workers leave their employers or the medical profession altogether, medical facilities may be left with a shortage of healthcare workers, which could mean longer working hours and more stress for those employees left behind. Burnout is high among surgeons [6] . For example, a survey of almost 8000 U.S. surgeons found that 40% were burned out, and 30% screened positive for symptoms of depression [7] . Long working hours, stressful working conditions, flawless work expectations, entrenched power hierarchies, litigation risks, and complicated payment systems all play a role.

Workplace discrimination and harassment contribute to a hostile work environment and can also lead to burnout among surgeons. In fact, evidence from 262 U.S. surgical residency programs in the U.S. indicates that residents who had experienced workplace mistreatment had about three times the odds of reporting burnout compared to surgical residents who had not experienced mistreatment [8] . Although gender-based discrimination was particularly high in this sample (65% of women and 10% of men surgeons reported that they had experienced gender discrimination), the study did not investigate the nature of the discrimination or the channels through which it was associated with burnout. More generally, a number of studies have found evidence of greater self-reported gender discrimination among women surgeons [9] , [10] , [11] , [12] , but to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have used econometric methods to investigate the relationship between gender bias and burnout among surgeons.

Our goal is to fill this gap with an investigation of the association between gender-based bias and burnout among surgeons using a sample from Korea, a country with a long history gender-based discrimination in the workplace. Korea has had persistently high gender earnings inequality and occupational segregation (some of the highest among all OECD members) and it has high rates of occupational stress and burnout in the healthcare sector [13] , [14] . Both these facets make Korea an important case study of the relationship between gender bias and burnout among surgeons. Our study uses a novel dataset to measure gender bias based on a detailed 15-question survey instrument that includes both formal and informal types of discriminatory treatment in the workplace. Formal types of bias include discrepancies in pay and promotion, while informal types of bias include differential access to resources and opportunities.

The primary aim of this study is to examine the association between experiences of gender bias and burnout among surgeons, including whether this association operates directly or indirectly through job satisfaction. Gender bias can be both formal (e.g., hiring, pay, promotion, and leadership opportunities) and informal (e.g., exclusion from networks, limited access to resources, derogatory or gendered comments). The secondary aim is to determine whether these associations differ between women and men surgeons, given that women and men may experience and respond to gender bias differently. Although prior studies have accumulated rich evidence of the existence of sexism and gender-based discrimination in medicine, the measures used in these studies are more limited in capturing the variance in types and situations [15] , [16] , [17] . Moreover, most previous research on gender bias in the healthcare sector has focused on women's experiences with bias. Men can also experience gender bias in the workplace. For example, expectations around masculinity may put pressure on men to work longer hours and spend less time with their families, which can take a toll on their personal lives. Better understanding how gender bias influences both women and men is critical for healthcare organizations to change their policies and practices, especially those related to the workplace environment.

Our analysis is based on a conceptual framework in which gender bias has both a direct association with burnout and an indirect association through job satisfaction ( Fig. 1 ). Consistent with the theoretical framework of job burnout in Janssen et al. [18] , gender bias can have a direct association with burnout through its influence on job demands and resources. We posit that gender bias also has an indirect association with burnout through job satisfaction. Previous studies have found that gender-based bias and discrimination significantly decrease job satisfaction [18] , [19] and job satisfaction is strongly inversely related to burnout [7] , [20] , [21] . Evidence from these studies provides support that job satisfaction, an important worker attribute, can serve as an indirect pathway through which gender bias is associated with burnout.

Conceptual framework.

Experience with gender discrimination and bias can have a direct negative association with surgeons' satisfaction with their job. Job satisfaction is an attitude formed through cognitive processes which can be influenced negatively by experiencing gender discrimination and bias. Job satisfaction in turn is a predictive factor of job burnout [22] . However, women's experiences with gender discrimination are likely to differ from those of men due to a male-dominant culture or ‘old boys' club’ mentality and various gender biases [23] , [24] , [25] , [26] , [27] . Women surgeons are likely to experience more gender discrimination than male surgeons throughout their careers, from medical school and surgical residency to surgical practice or as academics [15] , [28] .

Our analysis categorizes workplace discrimination into two types: formal and informal. Formal discrimination is evident in organizational procedures like hiring, firing, and compensation decisions. Informal discrimination (also referred to as interpersonal discrimination) involves negative verbal and nonverbal behaviors such as derogatory comments, inappropriate jokes, and spreading rumors about coworkers [29] . This subtler bias also includes lack of mentorship, exclusion from informal communication networks, and an unwelcoming workplace culture. These barriers may not be immediately apparent to everyone in the workplace environment, as men are notably less likely than women to recognize them [30] .

To examine the relationship between gender bias and job burnout, the research team conducted a survey of Korean surgeons using a novel survey instrument. We distributed the survey questionnaire via the web-based survey tool SurveyMonkey to all registered members of the Korean Surgical Society. The survey was conducted from November 11, 2019, to January 11, 2020, and the response rate was 9.5%, with 432 responses from a total 4525 affiliated surgeons. After excluding incomplete responses, our final sample included 378 respondents. The survey was conducted in the Korean language.

Gender bias, the key independent variable for this study, was measured with 15 binary-coded questions. The authors developed the items through the process of primary development, consultations with surgeons and subject matter experts, and a pilot test. The survey instrument used the Korean term “cha-byeol,” which is often used as an umbrella term for discrimination, bias, and disadvantage. Sample questions include whether the respondent was told they were not suited to surgery because of their gender, if they were asked about marriage and childbirth plans during their medical career, and if they believed that they experienced bias or exclusion related to applying for a fellowship or a residency.

The questionnaire was created through a multistep process. We first conducted a literature review of instruments used to assess gender discrimination in surgery, medicine, and human resource management. Based on this review, we generated an initial pool of items and then conducted in-depth interviews with eight female surgeons. Two experts (one in medical ethics and one in gender equality) reviewed the draft items, after which we revised and refined the questionnaire. A pilot test with the same eight surgeons and the two experts was used to confirm content validity, leading to the final set of items.

The original instrument consisted of 16 items on experiences of gender discrimination and 17 items on perceptions of gender discrimination. In this study, we used only the experience items. For the present analysis, two items capturing discrimination from non-surgeons (nursing/support staff and patients/caregivers) were excluded, and one item that had been omitted in the earlier study (“whether they were asked about marriage and childbirth plans during their medical career”) was added. As a result, the final scale comprised 15 items. The reliability (Cronbach's alpha) of these 15 items was 0.895. Each item was coded as “yes” = 1 and “no” = 0, and the sum of affirmative responses was used as the experience score. Formal gender bias consisted of nine items (range: 0–9), and informal gender bias consisted of six items (range: 0–6).

We differentiated between formal and informal gender bias following the criteria in Shen and Dhanani [29] . Bias which occurs in an organizational or work context is classified as formal, and bias which occurs in interpersonal relationships is classified as informal. The indicator included nine items on formal and six items on informal gender bias. The indicator for job satisfaction is based on a 21-item job satisfaction measure in Schroen et al. [31] , which we revised into a 12-item measure for this study. Lastly, we measured burnout with a two-item burnout indicator for medical professionals developed by West et al. [32] .

Other independent variables include years of practice, employment affiliation, and surgical sub-specialty. As shown in Table 1 , employment affiliation differentiates between academic positions (fellow, non-tenured, and tenured positions) and non-academic positions (hospital, private practice, and other). The surgical sub-specialties are clustered into five groups: upper GI; colorectal; hepatobiliary and pancreas; breast, thyroid, and endocrine; and pediatric, vascular, and general. We needed to form these broader categories due to having a small sample size and limited degrees of freedom, and this aggregation is consistent with commonly accepted categorizations of sub-specialties among Korean surgeons.

We estimated this set of equations with a mediation analysis using the SPSS Process tool [33] , with confidence intervals of 95%, and 5000 bootstrap iterations. For model verification, model 4 of Process v3.4 was applied using the SPSS Process Macro. The indirect association in the mediation model was verified for significance at the 95% confidence interval by applying the bootstrap method.

In addition to the control variables included in the final model (years of practice, employment affiliation, region, and surgical sub-specialty), we tested extended models that incorporated age, marital status, parental status, weekly work hours, and hospital type. These models exhibited multicollinearity due to high correlations among several variables. Therefore, we retained only one representative variable from each correlated cluster. In the final model, all variance inflation factor (VIF) values were below 3, with tolerance values ranging from 0.51 to 0.97 and VIF values ranging from 1.04 to 2.03, indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern.

An optional open-ended question was included after the 15 items assessing experiences of gender discrimination: “If you have experienced any other form of gender discrimination or disadvantage not covered by the above items, please describe it.” Responses to this question were analyzed descriptively to provide contextual information for the quantitative findings. Because these responses were not collected as part of a formal qualitative study and the number of responses was limited (n = 23), a formal qualitative coding procedure was not applied. Instead, the first author reviewed all responses and organized them into preliminary thematic categories. One co-author independently reviewed the responses and the proposed categories, and the final themes and representative quotations were agreed upon through discussion.

Results for our descriptive analysis are found in Table 2 , which reports the average incidence of each type of formal and informal gender bias for men and women. For all types of bias surveyed, the difference between men and women surgeons was statistically significant at the 0.01 level. As robustness checks, we additionally estimated (1) pooled regression models including gender-by-gender bias interaction terms, (2) models simultaneously including formal and informal gender bias, and (3) mediation models using 10,000 bootstrap samples. Across these analyses, the substantive conclusions remained unchanged. The gender-by-bias interaction terms were not statistically significant, indicating that the associations between gender bias and burnout did not differ significantly by gender. Simultaneous inclusion of formal and informal gender bias resulted in some attenuation of coefficient estimates but did not alter the overall pattern of findings. Increasing the number of bootstrap resamples from 5000 to 10,000 produced virtually identical indirect effect estimates and confidence intervals.

Table 3 provides the results of the mediation analysis for the association between formal gender bias and burnout, with an exploration of an indirect pathway through job satisfaction. The first stage results indicate that for both men and women surgeons, experiencing formal gender bias in the workplace is negatively associated with job satisfaction. Of note, the magnitude of this gender bias coefficient for job satisfaction is larger for men than for women. In the second stage regressions, for both men and women, job satisfaction is negatively associated with burnout. Hence, the more satisfied that a surgeon is with their job, the less likely they are to report burnout. Moreover, formal gender bias is statistically insignificant for both men and women surgeons, consistent with an indirect pathway between gender bias and burnout through job satisfaction. The other variables also yield some meaningful results. For both men and women, more years of practice are associated with heightened job satisfaction and less burnout. Working in academia is also associated with greater job satisfaction for both men and women compared with working in a hospital or private practice. There are no statistically significant associations of the surgical sub-specialties with job satisfaction or with burnout.

Years of practice are again associated with more job satisfaction for both men and women and with less burnout, although in this case the coefficient for women is not statistically significant. Working in academia is also associated with greater job satisfaction for both men and women compared to working in a hospital or a private practice. Finally, the surgical sub-specialties did not yield many meaningful results, except for the job satisfaction results for men: in this case, surgeons specializing in upper GI and in breast, thyroid, and endocrine surgeries have greater job satisfaction.

In the final part of the analysis, we disaggregate general job satisfaction into two categories: surgeons' satisfaction with their peer relationships, and surgeons' intellectual satisfaction. Results reveal that formal and informal gender bias have differential associations with subdimensions of men and women surgeons' job satisfaction, and in turn, these subdimensions of job satisfaction have differential associations with burnout across gender. The results for satisfaction with colleagues (Appendix Tables 1 and 2) indicate that experiencing formal and informal types of gender bias contributes to reduced satisfaction with one's colleagues for both men and women, which should not come as a surprise. However, satisfaction with one's colleagues does not influence women surgeons' burnout while it has a mitigating relationship with men surgeons' burnout. Moreover, informal gender bias has a positive association with job burnout only for men, and this association is consistent with only a partial indirect pathway through satisfaction with one's colleagues.

In the case of satisfaction with intellectual challenges, for men surgeons, formal gender bias has a negative association with intellectual satisfaction, while neither intellectual satisfaction nor formal gender bias have an association with burnout (Appendix Table 3). In contrast, for women, satisfaction with intellectual challenges matters for burnout, but not enough to completely mediate the negative association between formal gender bias and burnout. In the case of informal gender bias and satisfaction with intellectual challenges (Appendix Table 4), for both men and women, intellectual satisfaction serves to reduce job burnout, but this association is only statistically significant for women. For men, there is still a direct association between informal gender bias and burnout.

The open-ended questions also yielded some interesting observations about gender bias. Among the men surgeons, four respondents said that they had greater workloads or were blamed more often for mistakes specifically because they were men. Several men also responded that women patients only wanted a woman doctor to perform anal or breast surgeries. Also of note, one man reported being forced to participate in a drinking party against his will, and another man commented on the existence of a professional association for women doctors but not for men doctors.

Gender bias experienced by women often involved specific derogatory references to being a woman and personal humiliation, and it involved seeing pregnancy, childbirth, and childcare as a hindrance to women's job performance. The themes presented below were derived from an iterative review of the open-ended responses by two authors and are intended to illustrate the quantitative findings rather than to provide a comprehensive qualitative analysis. The first author developed preliminary categories, and one co-author reviewed both the original responses and the proposed categories. Any differences in interpretation were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. According to the open-ended responses, gender bias frequently included attaching ‘female’ to professions, not simply referring to them as ‘doctors’ but specifically as ‘female doctors.’ Some women reported that they got reactions from peers saying, “Women doctors lack sociability,” and “This is why women shouldn't do this” when they leave work on time due to childcare or find it difficult to participate in after-work gatherings. Among the women, five surgeons reported being insulted for making use of maternity leave, with remarks like “Why hire someone like that?” When adjusting schedules to exclude night shifts due to pregnancy, women heard comments like “It must be tough for the men doctors who work with you.” Many women doctors in our data were told that it is difficult to become professors because they marry, have children, and cannot work for long hours due to childcare. During job interviews, they were asked about pregnancy plans, and disclosing such plans to have a child was viewed unfavorably. For example, one woman surgeon reported that she was assigned to library work that was unrelated to her job duties.

This study has shown that in our sample of Korean surgeons, women experienced gender bias much more frequently than men in a variety of contexts. While about 25% of men surgeons experienced gender bias of either of the two types (formal or informal), approximately 85% of women surgeons experienced gender bias of either type. Because gender bias is so common among women, it is possible that there may not be sufficient variation in the data for women to pick up the full association between gender bias and their job satisfaction or burnout. In open-ended questions and in the interviews our team did for questionnaire development, women doctors perceived gender bias as something very prevalent and expected.

The high rates of gender bias experienced by women surgeons in Korea are consistent with previous evidence for other countries, especially the U.S., of gender-based disadvantages experienced by women physicians and surgeons in terms of promotion [23] , [34] , salary [35] , [36] , [37] , editorial board membership [38] , [39] , getting mentored [12] , and holding department chair positions [40] . In terms of journals' editorial board representation, 19% of editorial board members in the ten highest-impact general surgery journals were women [39] . Of all medical specialties, surgical journals ranked as the third lowest in terms of women editors-in-chief [38] .

Surgical specialties are not the most attractive career option for women medical students [41] , [42] . Women students reported more exposure to gender discrimination and sexual harassment during medical school than men students, and the gap of exposure is biggest in general surgery among medical specialties [43] . This gender discrimination, which is largely based on pervasive gender biases in surgery [41] , [44] , discourages women medical students from considering surgery as a career, and it negatively affects their satisfaction with their surgical training [16] , [28] , [45] , [46] . As a result, gender discrimination leads to less commitment of women to the surgery specialty.

This study has also found that for both men and women surgeons, when job satisfaction is used as a mediator, experience with formal gender bias is not directly associated with burnout. For both men and women surgeons, the evidence is consistent with an indirect pathway through job satisfaction. In terms of informal bias, the results are more nuanced. Experience with informal gender bias increases women doctors' burnout only through job satisfaction, but for men, informal gender bias directly increases burnout even when job satisfaction is considered. The mediation analysis implies that if we can mitigate the relationship between formal gender biases and job satisfaction, men and women surgeons may experience less burnout on the job. Addressing informal sources of gender bias will require additional measures if the goal is to reduce burnout. These results, for the most part, also applied when we examined particular components of job satisfaction, specifically satisfaction with one's colleagues and satisfaction with intellectual challenges on the job. For the latter, however, formal gender bias is associated with more burnout among women surgeons, and satisfaction with intellectual challenges at work does not appear to serve as an indirect pathway for this association.

Our findings demonstrate that gender bias (both formal and informal) shapes surgeons' job satisfaction and burnout in distinct ways for men and women. While women surgeons experience markedly higher levels of bias across nearly all domains, men show a stronger direct association between informal bias and burnout, suggesting that gender norms and role expectations exert pressure across the surgical workforce. Recognizing these differentiated pathways is essential for designing interventions that work to reduce burnout and improve overall well-being. The word “gender” is often associated with women, but in our analysis of Korean surgeons, men also appeared to be responsive to gender bias. Norms around masculinity appear to have influenced the workplace of the Korean surgeons we surveyed, with gendered expectations (such as men working excessively long hours and taking the blame for mistakes) shaping the work experiences of some of the 25% of men surgeons who reported gender bias.

Despite the robustness of our findings, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the survey response rate (9.5%) may raise concerns about response bias. Although this rate is modest, similar surveys administered to medical professionals through professional associations (such as dentists, physicians, and oncologists) have reported response rates between 10% and 18%. In addition, a comparable national survey on gender equality by Shin and Lee [14] recorded a 9.2% response rate. Unlike these studies, our survey was distributed to all members of the Korean Surgical Society regardless of their current practice status in order to ensure anonymity and voluntary participation. Given that online surveys were not yet widely used among surgeons at the time of data collection, and that Korean surveys conducted under similar conditions often yield response rates below 20%, we believe the response rate in this study is comparable to other research involving medical professionals.

Second, female surgeons were overrepresented in our sample (29.6%) compared with the national proportion of women in surgery (8.9%), according to the Korean Statistical Information Service. Because the Korean Surgical Society does not provide detailed membership statistics, we relied on national data for comparison. Matching the sample to the national gender distribution would have resulted in an insufficient number of female cases for meaningful analysis; thus, oversampling of women was unavoidable. Because topic interest increases survey participation [47] and women often exhibit higher survey response rates than men (e.g., [48] ), studies of gender discrimination may be vulnerable to selection bias if women who have a stronger interest in the issue are more likely to participate. Indeed, several male surgeons expressed discomfort with the survey topic, and some commented that they perceived reverse discrimination.

Third, the response pattern suggests that certain subgroups were less likely to participate. Surgeons in private practice and those aged 60 or older had lower response rates, whereas women, academic surgeons, physicians in large hospitals, and those in their 30s and 40s were more likely to respond. Private practitioners may have fewer opportunities to experience organizational gender bias, and older surgeons may rely on distant retrospective memories when reporting discrimination. Therefore, although the sample does not perfectly represent the entire surgical workforce, the responses may more accurately reflect the experiences of younger surgeons working within organizational settings.

A final limitation is that the data were collected in late 2019, immediately before the COVID-19 pandemic, and the surgical workforce, workplace culture, and gender-equity initiatives may have evolved since that time. As such, the findings should be interpreted with caution and understood as reflecting a pre-pandemic baseline rather than current conditions. Although more recent contextual factors may differ, the structural patterns observed in the data (particularly the gendered distribution of bias and its association with burnout) provide valuable historical evidence on long-standing issues in the surgical profession.

In closing, this study points to the need for healthcare systems to move beyond individual-level resilience programs and toward organizational strategies that reduce structural and interpersonal bias. Hospitals and professional associations can strengthen pay and promotion transparency, standardize evaluation criteria for hiring and leadership opportunities, and institute independent oversight mechanisms to monitor compliance with anti-discrimination policies. Additionally, workforce planning should account for predictable leave patterns, such as parental or medical leave, by funding temporary staffing pools, redistributing caseloads more equitably, and ensuring departments have adequate backup coverage so that no group of surgeons disproportionately absorbs additional workload. Institutions should invest in and expand structured diversity initiatives and leadership development pipelines that support women's advancement into surgical leadership positions, as these approaches appear to play a key role in improving gender representation [49] .

Reducing informal bias requires cultural and relational interventions. Health systems should adopt mandatory training on implicit bias, bystander intervention, and microaggressions, delivered not as one-off modules but as recurring professional development integrated into accreditation requirements. Creating mentorship pipelines, mixed-gender peer support groups, and structured opportunities for inclusive decision-making can counter exclusion from informal networks. Finally, national surgical societies and hospital leadership should collect and publicly report disaggregated data on gender bias, job satisfaction, and burnout to track progress and incentivize change. Implemented together, these system-level reforms can advance gender equity, reduce burnout, and strengthen the long-term sustainability of the surgical workforce.
