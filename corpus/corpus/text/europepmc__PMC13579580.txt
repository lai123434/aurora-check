Geriatric nursing assistants are essential to long-term care, and sustaining their work engagement is a persistent workforce challenge.

Self-determination theory distinguishes autonomous and controlled forms of work motivation that may have different implications for work functioning.

Most evidence has used variable-centred methods and therefore provides limited information about heterogeneous motivational configurations within workers.

Three profiles were identified with high classification quality: passive externally driven (5.2%), partially internalised (52.4%), and highly integrated (42.4%).

Work engagement increased markedly across the three profiles, with a large between-profile effect for total engagement.

All four perceived organisational-climate dimensions were entered simultaneously in the primary multinomial model; fair support was the only dimension associated with profile membership after adjustment for the other included variables.

China’s rapidly ageing population has intensified demand for formal long-term care, while the sector continues to face uneven service capacity, shortages of trained care workers, and concerns about care quality ( Feng et al., 2020 ). Geriatric nursing assistants constitute the principal frontline workforce in aged care institutions. Time-and-motion research shows that care aides devote a substantial proportion of their workday to direct care ( Mallidou et al., 2013 ), and nursing-assistant-related factors have been associated with resident satisfaction ( Li et al., 2021 ). Workforce instability remains a major concern: high staff turnover may signal compromised care quality ( Gandhi et al., 2021 ), and nursing-assistant turnover is a persistent management problem ( Miller et al., 2023 ). Decisions to remain in or leave long-term care work involve trade-offs among working conditions, organisational support, and occupational expectations ( McGilton et al., 2014 ). Care assistants may also experience social discrimination and limited occupational recognition ( Travers et al., 2020 ), occupational inequalities ( Campoy-Vila et al., 2025 ), emotional and financial strain ( Ecker et al., 2021 ), and demanding work with constrained resources ( Korbus et al., 2023 ). These conditions make the maintenance of a stable and engaged geriatric nursing-assistant workforce a particular challenge in the Chinese long-term care context.

Work engagement, defined as a positive and fulfilling work-related state characterised by vigor, dedication, and absorption ( Schaufeli et al., 2006 ), was the primary outcome of this study. Among geriatric nursing assistants, engagement may reflect sustained willingness and capacity to remain attentive, emotionally responsive, and involved in demanding care work. Lower engagement is relevant to broader workforce concerns, including burnout and job dissatisfaction ( Norful et al., 2024 ). Staff turnover may further threaten continuity of care and organisational stability. Identifying modifiable motivational and organisational correlates of engagement is therefore important for aged care workforce development.

Work motivation has long been recognised as an important topic in nursing workforce research ( Toode et al., 2011 ). Self-determination theory distinguishes the quality of work motivation rather than treating motivation only as a matter of overall intensity ( Gagné and Deci, 2005 ). Intrinsic motivation and identified regulation represent relatively autonomous reasons for working, whereas introjected regulation involves internal pressure, obligation, or self-worth contingencies, and external regulation reflects rewards, sanctions, or other external demands. These autonomous and controlled forms of motivation can coexist within the same worker and may have different implications for work functioning ( Cerasoli et al., 2014 ). Their configuration may depend partly on whether the work environment supports the basic psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness ( Van den Broeck et al., 2016 ).

Most workforce studies have used variable-centred methods to estimate average associations between separate motivation dimensions and work outcomes. Although informative, average effects cannot show whether distinct forms of motivation combine differently across workers. Latent profile analysis provides a person-centred extension of self-determination theory research by identifying subgroups with qualitatively different motivational configurations ( Spurk et al., 2020 ). It also permits comparison of engagement and associated characteristics across empirically derived profiles, thereby generating evidence that may be more useful for differentiated workforce support than a single average-score approach ( Gillet et al., 2020 ).

Fair support was examined as a key organisational resource. In this study, it refers to workers’ perceptions that managers act fairly, provide practical assistance, and help address work-related problems and resource needs. Conceptually, fair support may represent a need-supportive organisational condition within self-determination theory and a job resource within the Job Demands–Resources model ( Bakker and Demerouti, 2007 ). Despite a growing literature on work motivation profiles in general employee populations, to our knowledge no published study has used a person-centred approach to identify work motivation profiles among geriatric nursing assistants in Chinese aged care institutions. This study therefore addressed three research questions: (1) What latent work motivation profiles can be identified among geriatric nursing assistants? (2) Does the primary outcome, work engagement, differ across the identified profiles? and (3) Which sociodemographic, occupational, family, and perceived organisational factors are associated with profile membership?

This cross-sectional questionnaire study used data from a broader survey of geriatric nursing assistants working in Chinese aged care institutions. The report was prepared with reference to the STROBE statement for observational studies ( von Elm et al., 2008 ). Participants were recruited using convenience and snowball sampling from nine aged care institutions in Xi’an and Weinan, Shaanxi Province, China, between May and August 2025. The institutions were selected pragmatically from facilities accessible to the research team that provided institutional aged-care services, employed geriatric nursing assistants, and granted permission for participant recruitment; no probability-based institutional sampling frame was used.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) current employment as a geriatric nursing assistant in an aged care institution; (2) continuous employment for at least 6 months; and (3) willingness to participate and ability to provide informed consent. Participants were excluded if they were on leave or otherwise absent from work during the survey period. A total of 445 questionnaires were distributed through institutional and participant-referral channels, and 396 were returned, yielding a response rate of 89.0%. Of the returned questionnaires, 66 were excluded: 48 because of patterned responding, 13 because completion time was <2 min, and five because of clearly inconsistent sociodemographic information. The remaining 330 questionnaires constituted the final analytic sample, corresponding to 83.3% of returned questionnaires. The numbers of participants included from Institutions A to I were 17, 168, 2, 94, 16, 4, 10, 9, and 10, respectively.

There is no universally accepted single power formula or minimum sample size for latent profile analysis. Simulation evidence indicates that profile recovery depends jointly on sample size, the number and quality of indicators, class separation, and the size of the smallest class ( Nylund et al., 2007 ). The available analytic sample of 330 participants was considered suitable for an exploratory analysis using four continuous motivation indicators. Model adequacy was evaluated using multiple fit indices, classification quality, class size, and substantive interpretability rather than a fixed sample-size threshold. Because the smallest profile contained 17 participants, estimates involving this profile were interpreted cautiously.

General information questionnaire. A self-designed questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic and work-related information, including sex, age, education, years of work, institution type, vocational level, number of older adults cared for per day, working hours, monthly income, marital status, children’s status, family responsibilities, and family support for work. Education was recorded in five prespecified categories corresponding to commonly used stages of schooling in China: primary school and below, junior middle school, high school or technical secondary school, junior college, and bachelor’s degree and above. These categories were retained separately in the descriptive and profile-comparison analyses to preserve potential educational gradients.

Motivation at Work Scale. Work motivation was assessed using the Chinese wording of the Motivation at Work Scale. The original scale was developed by Gagné et al. ( Gagné et al., 2010 ). The wording used in the present study was adopted directly from Zhang et al. ( Zhang et al., 2016 ), who applied the four-regulation structure among Chinese employees; the present research team did not undertake a new translation. The scale comprises four theoretically distinct three-item subscales: intrinsic motivation, identified regulation, introjected regulation, and external regulation. Items are rated on a 7-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree), and each subscale score ranges from 3 to 21. Higher scores indicate higher levels of the corresponding motivation type. The item-mean score for each of the four subscales, rounded to two decimal places, was used as a separate indicator in the latent profile analysis. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.918 for intrinsic motivation, 0.917 for identified regulation, 0.881 for introjected regulation, and 0.767 for external regulation.

Utrecht Work Engagement Scale-9. Work engagement was measured using the validated Chinese version of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale-9 ( Fong and Ng, 2012 ). The scale includes nine items across three dimensions: vigor, dedication, and absorption. Items are rated from 0 (never) to 6 (always), yielding a total score from 0 to 54; higher scores indicate higher work engagement. The total work engagement score was treated as the primary outcome, and the three dimension scores were examined as secondary outcomes. In the present sample, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.917.

Organisational climate variables. An adapted version of the Nursing Organisational Climate Scale was used to assess perceived organisational climate among geriatric nursing assistants ( He et al., 2011 ). The original instrument comprised 24 items. To improve contextual relevance, wording referring specifically to nurses, nursing departments, and hospital nursing settings was revised to refer to geriatric nursing assistants and aged care institutions. Under the supervision of the research team, the applicability and comprehensibility of each item were reviewed in relation to geriatric nursing-assistant duties and aged-care management. Managers from participating aged care settings reviewed the adapted wording, and several geriatric nursing assistants provided feedback on clarity and contextual relevance. Two items considered to have limited relevance to geriatric nursing assistants’ work were omitted, resulting in a 22-item adapted version. No formal expert-panel content-validity assessment or quantitative content-validity index was conducted. The final scale comprised four dimensions: fair support (11 items), coworker behaviour (5 items), interpersonal climate (4 items), and learning and advancement climate (2 items). Fair support reflected perceived managerial fairness, practical support, and assistance with work-related problems and resources. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating a more favourable perceived organisational climate.

Before formal data collection, the complete questionnaire was pilot-tested among 30 geriatric nursing assistants from an aged care institution in Xi’an who met the eligibility criteria. The pilot assessed questionnaire clarity, comprehensibility, and feasibility. Minor wording adjustments were made to the general information questionnaire and the adapted organisational-climate scale; the scoring procedures were unchanged. Pilot data were not included in the final analysis.

Electronic questionnaires were administered using the Wenjuanxing platform. Before data collection, all investigators received standardised training on the study objectives, eligibility criteria, informed consent, questionnaire administration, and communication procedures. For the convenience-sampling component, after permission was obtained from each institution, an institutional liaison notified potentially eligible geriatric nursing assistants, distributed the questionnaire QR code, and arranged an appropriate location for questionnaire completion. For the snowball-sampling component, participants were permitted to forward the QR code through WeChat to other potentially eligible geriatric nursing assistants, whose eligibility and informed consent were confirmed before questionnaire completion. Institutional liaisons were not involved in participants’ responses and had no access to individual questionnaire data. Participants read the electronic informed-consent information before entering the questionnaire and indicated voluntary agreement to participate. All participants completed the questionnaire on their own mobile phones. When participants had difficulty reading items or operating a smartphone, trained investigators read or clarified instructions and assisted with device operation but did not suggest or select responses.

The platform permitted submission only through WeChat and restricted each device or internet protocol address to one submission. All questionnaire items were mandatory; therefore, submitted questionnaires contained no item-level missing data. No names, identification numbers, telephone numbers, employee numbers, or other direct identifiers were collected, and institutional managers could not view individual responses. Patterned responding was defined as selecting the same response option across an entire multi-item scale or displaying a regular alternating pattern across consecutive scale items. The completion-time criterion of <2 min was applied to all electronic submissions, including questionnaires completed with investigator assistance. Clearly inconsistent sociodemographic information referred to implausible contradictions among variables such as age, years of employment, marital status, and children’s status. The final dataset was also checked for extreme values and logical inconsistencies before analysis.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of a tertiary hospital in Northwest China (No. 2025-Lunshen-143). All participants provided informed consent and participated voluntarily. Personal information was kept confidential and used only for research purposes.

SPSS 26.0 and Mplus 8.0 were used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Percentages were calculated using the full analytic sample unless otherwise specified. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to describe the associations between the four work-motivation dimensions and total work engagement. Latent profile analysis was conducted using the item-mean scores of intrinsic motivation, identified regulation, introjected regulation, and external regulation, each rounded to two decimal places, as four continuous observed indicators. Models with one to five profiles were estimated. Model selection was informed by the Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion, adjusted Bayesian information criterion, entropy, Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test, bootstrap likelihood ratio test, class proportions, and theoretical interpretability. Lower information-criterion values indicated better relative fit; statistically significant likelihood-ratio tests supported a model with one additional profile; and higher entropy indicated more accurate classification ( Nylund et al., 2007 ).

After the optimal profile solution was selected, differences in participant characteristics across profiles were examined using chi-square tests; Fisher’s exact test was used when the expected frequencies did not meet the assumptions of the chi-square test. The distributions of total work motivation and the four work-motivation dimensions within the identified profiles were examined using descriptive statistics and graphical inspection, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. Because homogeneity of variance was not supported for several work-motivation scores, Welch analysis of variance and Kruskal-Wallis tests were conducted as sensitivity analyses. Because the four motivation dimensions were used as indicators to derive the latent profiles, the between-profile comparisons were interpreted primarily as descriptive characterisation of the profile configurations rather than as independent confirmatory tests. The distributions of total work engagement and its dimensions within profiles were assessed using histograms, quantile-quantile plots, skewness, and kurtosis; homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Group differences were examined using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference tests, and eta squared was reported as the effect-size measure. Welch analysis of variance and Kruskal-Wallis tests were conducted as sensitivity analyses. Multinomial logistic regression was used to explore factors associated with profile membership, with the highly integrated profile as the reference group. Based on theoretical relevance, bivariate findings, and category distributions, the primary model simultaneously included age, education, vocational level, household registration, children’s status, family support for work, and the original total scores of all four perceived organisational-climate dimensions: fair support, coworker behaviour, interpersonal climate, and learning and advancement climate. Results are reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

This study was not registered.

As the number of profiles increased, the AIC, BIC, and adjusted BIC values generally decreased, indicating improved model fit. The two-, three-, and four-profile models showed statistically significant LMR and BLRT values, suggesting that adding one more profile improved fit compared with the preceding model.

Statistically significant differences were also observed for intrinsic motivation, identified regulation, introjected regulation, and external regulation. Participants in the highly integrated profile had the highest scores across all four dimensions, whereas those in the passive externally driven profile had the lowest scores. Levene’s test supported homogeneity of variance for intrinsic motivation ( p = 0.308), but not for total work motivation ( p = 0.004), identified regulation, introjected regulation, or external regulation (all p < 0.001). Nevertheless, Welch analyses of variance and Kruskal-Wallis sensitivity analyses confirmed statistically significant between-profile differences in total work motivation and all four dimensions (all p < 0.001). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons yielded the same ordering, with scores consistently following the C3 > C2 > C1 pattern. Because the four motivation dimensions were used to derive the latent profiles, these comparisons were interpreted primarily as descriptive characterisation of the profile configurations.

Work engagement showed statistically significant differences across the three latent profiles. Participants in the highly integrated profile had the highest total work engagement score, followed by those in the partially internalised profile, whereas those in the passive externally driven profile had the lowest score. Similar gradient patterns were observed for vigor, dedication, and absorption. Post hoc comparisons showed statistically significant differences between every pair of profiles, with the ordering C3 > C2 > C1. Overall, work engagement followed a clear C3 > C2 > C1 pattern ( Table 6 ). Levene’s tests did not indicate heterogeneity of variance for total work engagement, vigor, dedication, or absorption ( p = 0.110–0.874). Using a person-centred approach, this study identified three work motivation profiles among geriatric nursing assistants: a passive externally driven profile (5.2%), a partially internalised profile (52.4%), and a highly integrated profile (42.4%). Work engagement showed a clear graded pattern across the profiles, with the highest scores in the highly integrated profile and the lowest scores in the passive externally driven profile. Fair support was the organisational factor most consistently associated with profile membership when all four perceived organisational-climate dimensions and the selected individual and family covariates were entered simultaneously. These findings address the study’s three research questions while highlighting heterogeneity that would not be visible from average motivation scores alone.

The three-profile solution indicates that geriatric nursing assistants combine autonomous and controlled reasons for working in different ways. The passive externally driven profile had low intrinsic motivation, identified regulation, and introjected regulation, while external regulation was relatively more prominent within an otherwise low-scoring configuration. This pattern may characterise workers who experience limited personal meaning, weak identification with care work, and reliance on external requirements or immediate economic necessity. Because this profile included only 17 participants, it should be regarded as a small and potentially vulnerable subgroup rather than a precisely estimated population category.

The partially internalised profile was the largest group and showed moderate scores across all four motivation types. Its prevalence may reflect the mixed realities of frontline long-term care in China: workers may value helping older adults and recognise the social usefulness of their work, while also responding to family responsibilities, income needs, occupational expectations, and managerial requirements. The profile should not be interpreted as a developmental stage or as evidence that workers will naturally progress toward another profile. Rather, it represents the most common motivational configuration observed in this cross-sectional sample. Its size suggests that workforce policies focused only on a small low-motivation group would overlook a substantial number of workers whose engagement may remain sensitive to everyday feedback, recognition, workload fairness, and opportunities to develop competence.

The highly integrated profile showed high scores on all four measured motivation types. The label is descriptive and does not imply that integrated regulation, a distinct construct in self-determination theory, was directly measured. The coexistence of high intrinsic and identified motivation with high introjected and external regulation suggests a high-scoring mixed-motivation configuration rather than a purely autonomous form of motivation. Workers in this profile may find care work meaningful while also experiencing strong duty, self-imposed pressure, and responsiveness to organisational expectations. Such controlled components should not be treated as uniformly beneficial: introjected regulation can sustain effort in the short term but may also involve guilt, obligation, or contingent self-worth. The relatively large size of this profile may partly reflect selective retention of workers who can reconcile the emotional meaning of care with demanding institutional expectations; longitudinal evidence is needed to test this interpretation.

Work engagement differed substantially across the three profiles, and the between-profile effect sizes were large for total engagement, vigor, dedication, and absorption. The same ordering was obtained in parametric and robust sensitivity analyses. This pattern is consistent with the proposition that the configuration of work motives is relevant to how much physical, cognitive, and emotional energy workers report investing in their roles. However, the cross-sectional design does not establish direction: more engaged workers may also interpret their reasons for working more positively or report stronger motivation across several dimensions.

Self-determination theory provides one explanation for the observed gradient. Work environments that allow workers to exercise appropriate discretion, develop care competence, and experience respectful relationships may support the needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness ( Van den Broeck et al., 2016 ). Satisfaction of these needs is conceptually compatible with stronger intrinsic motivation and identified regulation and with sustained engagement in demanding care work. At the same time, the high-engagement profile also contained strong introjected and external regulation, underscoring that engagement can coexist with obligation and pressure. This nuance is important because high current engagement does not necessarily indicate low strain or long-term well-being. Research among healthcare assistants has linked adverse work conditions with burnout and poorer well-being ( Norful et al., 2024 ), while a recent study of nurses in a resource-constrained, high-stress setting similarly showed that engagement was associated with psychological and demographic resources ( Aqtam et al., 2025 ). The present study did not measure burnout or turnover intention, so these outcomes should be examined directly in future profile-based research.

Fair support was the most consistent organisational correlate of profile membership. Higher perceived fair support was associated with lower odds of belonging to the passive externally driven or partially internalised profile rather than the highly integrated profile. All four perceived organisational-climate dimensions were entered simultaneously in the primary multinomial model; coworker behaviour, interpersonal climate, and learning and advancement climate were not statistically associated with profile membership after adjustment. The Job Demands–Resources model conceptualises managerial fairness, practical assistance, and access to problem-solving resources as job resources that may help workers manage high demands ( Bakker and Demerouti, 2007 ). From a self-determination theory perspective, fair procedures and respectful managerial support may contribute to the satisfaction of workers’ needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. These theoretical interpretations remain non-causal: engaged or highly motivated workers may perceive management more favourably, and unmeasured staffing or leadership factors may influence both perceptions and profile membership.

The finding is also plausible within the wider East Asian long-term care context. In Korean long-term care facilities, person-centred work environments have been associated with greater job satisfaction and lower turnover intention among care staff ( Choi and Kim, 2021 ), and organisational support, training, autonomy, and employee participation have been linked to care-worker commitment and turnover intention ( Ha et al., 2014 ). Japanese evidence shows that care assistants derive different work benefits from tasks involving social contribution, relationships, learning, and income ( Sugiura et al., 2024 ). Recent evidence from Taiwan has likewise identified managerial fairness, willingness to listen to staff, and supportive internal services as priorities in long-term care workforce management ( Chang and Huang, 2026 ). Together, these studies suggest that organisational resources and the perceived meaning of care work are relevant across ageing Asian societies, although differences in financing, occupational regulation, staffing, and facility governance limit direct comparison.

Vocational level, children’s status, and family support were associated with profile membership in selected comparisons, but these estimates should be interpreted cautiously because the smallest profile and some subcategories contained few participants. Limited vocational training may coincide with fewer opportunities to experience competence, occupational recognition, or advancement. Family support may reduce conflict between care work and responsibilities outside work, whereas family demands may strengthen both identified reasons for earning an income and controlled pressure to remain employed. These explanations are provisional. The absence of statistical evidence for several other demographic variables should not be interpreted as proof that they are irrelevant; limited power for sparse subgroup comparisons and unmeasured contextual factors remain possible.

The results support differentiated rather than uniform workforce support. Institutional managers could first identify workers reporting low motivation and low fair support through confidential, non-punitive staff assessment. For workers resembling the passive externally driven profile, practical options include a structured orientation programme, assignment of a trained mentor, regular supervisory check-ins, review of workload and role clarity, and access to basic vocational certification. These actions should address immediate barriers and competence needs rather than labelling individuals as deficient.

The partially internalised profile is the numerically largest target group. Managers and nursing educators could offer modular skills training, timely performance feedback, recognition of care contributions, opportunities to participate in care-planning or quality-improvement discussions, and transparent pathways from entry-level to advanced vocational qualifications. For workers in the highly integrated profile, the priority is not simply to increase motivation further. Workload monitoring, adequate rest, emotional debriefing, and protection from chronic overcommitment are warranted because high engagement may coexist with internal pressure and strong duty.

At organisational level, fair support can be operationalised through published scheduling rules, transparent workload allocation, consistent performance appraisal, accessible grievance procedures, respectful communication, and documented follow-up when staff raise care or resource problems. Provincial and national policymakers could support these practices through clearer occupational standards, subsidised continuing education, portable certification, and workforce-funding mechanisms that allow facilities to provide training and supervisory time. Implementation should be adapted to ownership, staffing, size, and local resource capacity; the present study was not designed to determine which strategy is most effective in public versus private, urban versus rural, or large versus small facilities. Longitudinal and intervention studies are required before these proposals can be regarded as effective methods for changing profile membership or engagement.

This study focused on an essential but under-researched long-term care workforce and used latent profile analysis to examine combinations of autonomous and controlled motivation. The classification quality of the three-profile solution was high, and profile differences in engagement were supported by large effect sizes and robust group-comparison sensitivity analyses. The study also examined individual, family, occupational, and perceived organisational correlates, with all four perceived organisational-climate dimensions entered simultaneously in the primary multinomial model.

Several limitations require consideration. First, all variables were measured concurrently, so temporal order, causal mechanisms, and transitions between profiles cannot be inferred. Reverse associations and alternative explanations are plausible. Second, convenience and snowball recruitment from nine institutions in Xi’an and Weinan limits transferability to other regions of China, particularly rural settings and areas with different long-term care financing, labour markets, and workforce regulations. Institution sizes were highly unbalanced, and only nine institutions were included, limiting stable estimation of institution-level effects. Third, although 396 of 445 distributed questionnaires were returned (89.0%), the characteristics of nonrespondents were not available; nonresponse bias and selection bias related to non-probability recruitment therefore cannot be excluded. Fourth, reliance on self-report questionnaires may have introduced social desirability, recall, and common-method bias. Fifth, the organisational-climate instrument was contextually adapted and pilot-tested, but no formal expert-panel content-validity assessment or quantitative content-validity index was undertaken. Sixth, the passive externally driven profile contained only 17 participants, and estimates for sparse demographic categories and multinomial regression comparisons may be unstable. Finally, latent profiles are model- and sample-dependent; the labels are descriptive, and replication with larger, geographically diverse samples is needed. Prospective studies and latent transition analysis should examine profile stability and change, while intervention studies should test whether specific managerial practices alter motivation or engagement.

Among 330 geriatric nursing assistants from nine aged care institutions in two cities in Shaanxi Province, three work motivation profiles were identified, and work engagement differed markedly across them. Fair support was the organisational factor most consistently associated with membership in the more highly motivated profile, but the cross-sectional findings do not establish causation or national representativeness. Institutional managers should consider confidential identification of workers with low motivation or low fair support, structured orientation and mentoring, transparent workload allocation, consistent appraisal and grievance procedures, and differentiated vocational-development pathways. Because facility ownership, staffing, size, and regional resources differ, these recommendations should be adapted rather than applied uniformly across public and private, urban and rural, or large and small institutions. Nursing educators and policymakers can support these efforts through accessible certification and continuing education. The feasibility and effectiveness of these strategies, and whether workers move between profiles over time, should be evaluated in longitudinal and intervention research across more diverse Chinese long-term care settings.
